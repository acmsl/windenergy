Grid Electrical Connection

What is the Grid Connection?

The grid connection is where the wind turbine delivers its electrical energy to the power system. Think of it as the "delivery point" - all that energy captured from wind, converted to mechanical rotation, then to electricity, finally leaves the turbine here.

But it's not as simple as just plugging in a power cord! The connection involves multiple stages and components, each critical for safe, reliable operation.

Two-Stage Connection System

The electrical connection actually happens in two distinct stages:

Stage 1: Turbine to Wind Farm Substation

  • Voltage: Medium voltage (15 kV or 22 kV typically)
  • Distance: Can be hundreds of meters to several kilometers
  • Cable: Underground cable in wind farms
  • Purpose: Collect power from individual turbines

Stage 2: Substation to Transmission Grid

  • Voltage: High voltage (110 kV, 132 kV, or higher)
  • Distance: Can be many kilometers
  • Connection: Overhead lines or underground cable
  • Purpose: Feed power into main electrical grid

Why two stages?

Efficiency:

  • Higher voltage = lower current for same power
  • Lower current = less power lost in cables (I²R losses)
  • 690V from generator → huge current → massive cable losses
  • 15kV or 22kV → much smaller current → acceptable losses

Practicality:

  • Can't step directly from 690V to 110kV+ (transformer too large/expensive)
  • Medium voltage is perfect intermediate step
  • Allows reasonable transformer size at each turbine

Flexibility:

  • Multiple turbines feed into common substation
  • Substation aggregates power from entire wind farm
  • Single connection point to main grid
  • Easier for grid operator to manage

Stage 1: Inside the Wind Turbine

Let's trace the power path from generator to the turbine's connection point:

Starting Point: The Generator

In the turbine we're simulating:

  • Type: Squirrel cage induction generator
  • Output voltage: 690 V AC (three-phase)
  • Frequency: 50 Hz (or 60 Hz depending on grid)
  • Power: Up to 1,300 kW (when using Large Generator, 4-pole)

The Power Cables

These carry power from nacelle to tower base:

Physical routing:

  1. Exit generator at top of nacelle
  2. Hang freely for 5-10 meters (allowing nacelle rotation)
  3. Clamp to tower wall
  4. Run down inside tower
  5. Enter electrical cabinet at tower base

Cable specifications:

  • Three conductors (one per phase)
  • Plus grounding conductor
  • Cross-section: Typically 150-240 mm² per conductor
  • Insulation rated for 1 kV minimum
  • Must handle 1,000+ amps
  • Must be flexible (allow nacelle rotation)

The twist limitation:

  • Remember: these cables can only twist ±2.5 turns
  • Limits how much nacelle can rotate
  • Requires periodic "untwisting" procedure

The Electrical Panel (at Tower Base)

This is where the magic happens - let's look inside:

[Reference to detailed circuit diagram]

Main Components:

1. Main Circuit Breaker ("Interruptor Principal")

The master switch for the entire turbine:

Function:

  • Connects/disconnects turbine from wind farm grid
  • Can be operated manually (maintenance)
  • Can be operated by Controller (emergency shutdown)

States:

  • ON (Closed): Power can flow
    • Turbine can operate normally
    • Grid voltage present on turbine side
  • OFF (Open): No power flow
    • Turbine is isolated
    • Safe for maintenance work
    • Cannot produce (no grid connection)

Safety feature:

  • Manual operation: Can be turned ON or OFF by technician
  • Remote operation: Controller can open it (but not close it!)
    • Why? Safety - prevents automatic restart during maintenance
    • Human must physically close it to re-enable operation

2. Busbars

Think of these as "electrical highways":

What they are:

  • Thick copper or aluminum bars
  • One per phase (three total)
  • Plus neutral and ground bars

Purpose:

  • Distribute power to multiple circuits
  • Connect incoming power to outgoing power
  • Provide measurement points
  • Allow branching to auxiliary systems

Why use busbars instead of just wire?

  • Can handle very high currents (1,000+ amps)
  • Easy to add/remove connections
  • Excellent heat dissipation
  • Visible inspection possible
  • Industry standard for power distribution

3. Current Transformers (CTs)

These measure how much current is flowing:

What they are:

  • Toroidal (donut-shaped) cores
  • Power conductor passes through center
  • Secondary winding provides measurement signal

How they work:

The principle is electromagnetic induction:

  1. Primary: Main power cable passes through CT center (creates magnetic field)
  2. Secondary: Small winding wrapped around toroid (senses magnetic field)
  3. Ratio: Typically 1000:5 or 2000:5
    • 1000 amps on primary → 5 amps on secondary
    • 2000 amps on primary → 5 amps on secondary

Why this ratio?

  • Measuring 1000+ amps directly is difficult/dangerous
  • 5 amps is safe, easy to measure with standard instruments
  • Controller reads the 5A signal
  • Multiplies by ratio (200:1 or 400:1) to get actual current

Installed location:

  • One CT per phase (three total)
  • Mounted on busbars before any branching
  • Measures total power output

Accuracy:

  • Class 0.5 or better (0.5% error maximum)
  • Critical for:
    • Revenue metering (how much energy sold)
    • Power quality monitoring
    • Overload protection
    • Performance optimization

4. Fuses

Overcurrent protection:

Purpose:

  • Protect equipment from overcurrent
  • Faster than circuit breakers
  • Last line of defense

Ratings:

  • Sized for 125-150% of normal maximum current
  • Typical: 1,600 - 2,000 amp fuses
  • Must coordinate with upstream protection

Location:

  • Between main breaker and busbars
  • One per phase

5. Thyristors and Bypass Contactors

We've covered these before, but let's see them in the electrical panel context:

The thyristor assembly:

  • Six thyristors total (two per phase, in anti-parallel)
  • Mounted on large heat sinks
  • Cooling fans often included
  • Temperature sensors monitoring each thyristor

The bypass contactors:

  • Three contactors (one per phase)
  • Mounted in parallel with thyristor pairs
  • Heavy-duty: must handle full load current continuously
  • Rated for 1,000+ amps

Control circuit:

  • Each thyristor has gate connection
  • Six gate signals from Controller
  • Timing is critical (must fire at precise moments)
  • See Soft Starter section for detailed operation

Temperature monitoring:

  • Bimetallic sensors on each thyristor
  • Connected in series
  • If ANY thyristor overheats:
    • Series circuit opens
    • "Over Temp" signal goes to Controller
    • Controller initiates shutdown

6. Generator Connection Contactors

These switch between the two generator configurations:

Small Generator Contactor (6-pole):

  • Connects stator winding for 6-pole operation
  • Synchronous speed: 1,000 RPM (at 50 Hz)
  • Used for lower wind speeds

Large Generator Contactor (4-pole):

  • Connects stator winding for 4-pole operation
  • Synchronous speed: 1,500 RPM (at 50 Hz)
  • Used for higher wind speeds / maximum power

Critical interlock:

  • NEVER both closed simultaneously!
  • Hardware interlock prevents this
  • Software also prevents it (defense in depth)
  • If both closed: would short-circuit generator windings!

Status feedback:

  • Each contactor has two auxiliary contacts:
    • NC (Normally Closed): Closed when contactor is OFF
    • NO (Normally Open): Closed when contactor is ON
  • Both signals go to Controller
  • Allows fault detection (see below)

Contactor Status Detection Logic

This is clever engineering - let's understand it:

The Challenge:

How does the Controller know for certain:

  • Is the contactor really closed?
  • Is the contactor really open?
  • Are the status wires broken?
  • Are the auxiliary contacts failed?

The Solution: XOR Logic

[Fig 1: Contactor status detection circuit]

The setup:

Each contactor has two auxiliary contacts:

  • NC contact: Provides +24V when contactor is OFF (open)
  • NO contact: Provides +24V when contactor is ON (closed)

These are mechanically linked to the main contactor - they MUST be in opposite states.

Signals to Controller:

Two signals per contactor:

  1. "Estado" (Status): From NC contact
    • Voltage present → contactor is OPEN
    • No voltage → contactor is CLOSED
  2. "OK/Fallo" (OK/Fault): XOR of both contacts
    • Logic: NC XOR NO
    • Should ALWAYS be high if system is healthy

Fault detection:

If OK/Fallo = 0:

Could be:

  1. Power supply failure:
    • +24V supply is off
    • Both NC and NO read 0
    • Controller knows: "Can't trust status, system problem"
  2. Both wires broken:
    • Wiring fault
    • Both NC and NO read 0
    • Same result: "Can't trust status"
  3. Auxiliary contact mechanism failed:
    • Both showing same state (impossible mechanically)
    • Either both reading 1 (both closed - mechanically impossible)
    • Controller knows: "Contactor status unreliable"

Controller action on fault:

If OK/Fallo goes to 0:

  • Don't trust the status signal
  • Assume worst case
  • Initiate shutdown
  • Create alarm: "Contactor Status Fault - needs maintenance"
  • Require manual inspection before restart

Why this is brilliant:

Simple two-wire system provides:

  • ✓ Status information (open/closed)
  • ✓ Self-checking (knows when status is unreliable)
  • ✓ Wire break detection
  • ✓ Power supply monitoring
  • ✓ Auxiliary contact fault detection

All with just basic logic!

Voltage and Current Measurement

The Controller needs to know what's happening electrically:

Voltage Measurement:

What's measured:

  • Voltage on each phase (Phase 1, 2, 3)
  • Phase-to-neutral voltage
  • All three phases monitored independently

Measurement method:

  • Voltage transformers (VTs) or voltage dividers
  • Step down 690V to safe measurement level (0-10V typically)
  • Analog-to-digital converter in Controller

What the Controller checks:

Per-phase:

  • Voltage present? (above minimum threshold ~600V)
  • Voltage in range? (620-760V typical)
  • Stable? (not fluctuating excessively)

All phases together:

  • All three phases present?
  • Balanced? (all within 5-10% of each other)
  • Phase sequence correct? (1-2-3 rotation)

Actions on voltage faults:

  • One phase lost: Immediate disconnect
    • Single-phasing damages generator
    • Creates unbalanced currents
    • Overheats windings rapidly
  • Under-voltage: Disconnect after brief delay
    • Might be momentary grid disturbance
    • Wait 2-5 seconds to see if recovers
    • If persists, disconnect
  • Over-voltage: Disconnect immediately
    • Can damage generator insulation
    • Might indicate grid fault
    • Safety priority

Current Measurement:

From the Current Transformers:

  • Three CTs (one per phase)
  • Output: 0-5A AC signal
  • Ratio: Typically 1000:5 (200:1 ratio)

What the Controller calculates:

From current and voltage measurements:

Active Power (P):

  • The "real" power doing useful work
  • Formula: P = V × I × cos(φ) × √3 (for three-phase)
  • Units: Watts (W) or Kilowatts (kW)
  • This is what gets sold/billed

Reactive Power (Q):

  • The "imaginary" power (out of phase)
  • Formula: Q = V × I × sin(φ) × √3
  • Units: VAR (Volt-Amp-Reactive) or kVAR
  • Doesn't do work, but necessary for grid

Apparent Power (S):

  • The total power (vector sum of P and Q)
  • Formula: S = V × I × √3
  • Units: VA (Volt-Amps) or kVA

Power Factor (cos φ):

  • Ratio of active to apparent power
  • Formula: PF = P / S = cos(φ)
  • Dimensionless (0 to 1, or percentage)
  • Grid operators care about this!

Why power factor matters:

  • High PF (>0.95): Efficient, grid operators happy
  • Low PF (<0.85): Inefficient, wastes grid capacity
  • Wind farms often required to maintain PF > 0.90
  • Can use capacitor banks or power electronics to improve

Current monitoring uses:

Overload protection:

  • Each phase current monitored
  • Exceeds 110% of rated? → Warning
  • Exceeds 120% of rated? → Reduce power
  • Exceeds 150% of rated? → Emergency disconnect

Imbalance detection:

  • Compare currents on all three phases
  • Should be within ~5% of each other
  • Large imbalance indicates problem:
    • Phase fault
    • Generator winding problem
    • Grid imbalance

Performance monitoring:

  • Power output vs. wind speed
  • Efficiency tracking
  • Trend analysis (declining performance?)

The Step-Up Transformer

After the electrical panel, power goes to the transformer:

Purpose:

Convert from generator voltage to grid collection voltage:

  • Input: 690V (from generator)
  • Output: 15 kV or 22 kV (wind farm standard)

Why step up the voltage?

It's all about reducing losses in the cables!

The physics:

Power loss in cables: P_loss = I² × R

Where:

  • I = current flowing
  • R = resistance of cable

Example calculation:

Wind turbine producing 1,300 kW:

At 690V (without transformer):

  • Current: I = P / (V × √3) = 1,300,000 / (690 × 1.732) = 1,088 amps!
  • Cable to substation: 2 km, resistance: 0.1 ohms
  • Loss: I² × R = (1,088)² × 0.1 = 118 kW lost!
  • That's 9% of total power lost as heat!

At 15 kV (with transformer):

  • Current: I = 1,300,000 / (15,000 × 1.732) = 50 amps
  • Same cable: 0.1 ohms
  • Loss: (50)² × 0.1 = 0.25 kW lost
  • Only 0.02% lost!

Huge difference!

This is why transformers are essential - they make long-distance power transmission practical.

Transformer Location:

Can be in two places:

Inside the nacelle:

  • Some turbine designs
  • Advantages:
    • Shorter 690V cable run
    • Less current in cables through tower
  • Disadvantages:
    • Weight in nacelle (structural load)
    • Heat in nacelle (cooling challenges)
    • More difficult to maintain

At tower base: (More common, including this simulator)

  • Adjacent to electrical panel
  • Advantages:
    • Easy to access for maintenance
    • Better cooling (more space, ground-level air)
    • Simpler nacelle design
  • Disadvantages:
    • Long 690V cable run (must handle high current)
    • Must twist with nacelle rotation

Transformer specifications:

Rating:

  • Sized for maximum turbine power
  • Typically 1,500 kVA (for 1,300 kW turbine)
  • Slightly oversized for reliability

Type:

  • Oil-filled (for cooling)
  • Or dry-type (newer designs, easier maintenance)

Taps:

  • Adjustable turns ratio
  • Allows fine-tuning output voltage
  • ±5% adjustment typical

Cooling:

  • Oil-filled: Natural convection (ONAN)
  • Or forced air (ONAF) for higher power
  • Radiators with cooling fans

Protection:

  • Temperature sensors
  • Pressure relief valves (oil-filled types)
  • Buchholz relay (detects internal faults)

From Transformer to Wind Farm Grid

After voltage step-up:

Medium Voltage Cable:

  • Underground cable (wind farms)
  • Three conductors plus ground
  • Cross-section: 95-240 mm² per conductor
  • Insulation rated for 15 kV or 22 kV

Routing:

  • Exits transformer
  • Goes underground in trench
  • Runs to wind farm collection point
  • Distance: Varies (100m to several km)

Wind Farm Collection System:

Multiple turbines connect to common "collection point":

  • Could be a junction box
  • Or directly to substation
  • All turbines' cables come together

To the Substation:

The wind farm substation:

  • Aggregates power from all turbines
  • Steps up voltage again (15/22 kV → 110/132 kV+)
  • Contains:
    • Multiple transformers
    • Circuit breakers
    • Protection systems
    • Metering equipment
    • Control systems

Finally to the Main Grid:

From substation:

  • High voltage transmission lines
  • Or underground cables (offshore wind)
  • Connects to transmission grid
  • Power enters electrical system
  • Distributed to consumers

Grid Connection Requirements

Wind farms must meet strict grid requirements:

Voltage Regulation:

  • Must maintain voltage within ±5% of nominal
  • Not cause voltage fluctuations
  • May require reactive power compensation

Frequency:

  • Must match grid frequency exactly
  • Cannot cause frequency variations
  • Must disconnect if grid frequency out of range

Power Quality:

  • Low harmonic distortion
  • Minimal flicker
  • Good power factor (typically > 0.90)

Fault Ride-Through:

  • Modern requirements: must stay connected during grid faults
  • Can't just disconnect when grid has problems
  • Must support grid stability
  • Called "LVRT" - Low Voltage Ride Through

Reactive Power:

  • Often required to provide reactive support
  • Helps grid voltage stability
  • May need power factor correction equipment

Two Operating Modes

The generator can connect in two different configurations:

Large Generator Mode (4-pole, 1500 RPM sync):

Stator winding configured for:

  • 2 pole pairs (4 poles total)
  • Synchronous speed: 50 Hz × 60 sec / 2 pairs = 1,500 RPM

When used:

  • Wind speed: 6.6 m/s to 25 m/s
  • Power output: Up to 1,300 kW (maximum)
  • Most energy production happens in this mode

Electrical characteristics:

  • Better power factor at high power
  • Higher efficiency
  • Lower current per kW generated

Small Generator Mode (6-pole, 1000 RPM sync):

Stator winding configured for:

  • 3 pole pairs (6 poles total)
  • Synchronous speed: 50 Hz × 60 sec / 3 pairs = 1,000 RPM

When used:

  • Wind speed: 3.5 m/s to 6.6 m/s
  • Power output: Up to ~350 kW
  • Extends operating range to lower winds

Electrical characteristics:

  • Different power factor curve
  • Optimized for lower power levels
  • Better match to TSR at low winds

How the winding connection changes:

Inside the generator:

  • Stator has special winding design
  • Can be connected two different ways
  • Contactors switch the connections
  • Creates different number of magnetic poles

Think of it like:

  • Same coils, different wiring pattern
  • Changes the magnetic field structure
  • Results in different synchronous speed

Safety Systems in Electrical Connection

Multiple layers of protection:

1. Main Circuit Breaker:

  • Can be opened manually (maintenance safety)
  • Can be opened by Controller (emergency)
  • Physical disconnect

2. Fuses:

  • Fast-acting overcurrent protection
  • Protect against short circuits
  • Last line of defense

3. Thyristor Temperature Monitoring:

  • Prevents semiconductor damage
  • Early warning of cooling problems

4. Voltage Monitoring:

  • All three phases continuously checked
  • Out of range → disconnect
  • Protects generator from grid faults

5. Current Monitoring:

  • Overload protection
  • Phase imbalance detection

6. Controller Logic:

  • Comprehensive fault detection
  • Safe shutdown sequences
  • Interlock enforcement

7. Emergency Stop:

  • Physical button
  • Immediately opens main breaker
  • Cannot be overridden by software

Maintenance Considerations

The electrical system requires periodic maintenance:

Visual Inspections (Monthly):

  • Check for:
    • Overheating signs (discoloration)
    • Loose connections
    • Corrosion
    • Insulation damage
    • Unusual odors

Contact Cleaning (Annually):

  • Contactors and breakers
  • Remove carbon buildup
  • Check for pitting/wear
  • Replace if degraded

Transformer Maintenance:

  • Oil level check (quarterly)
  • Oil analysis (annually)
  • Temperature monitoring
  • Leaks or damage inspection

Cable Inspections:

  • Termination tightness
  • Insulation condition
  • Support system integrity

Protection System Testing:

  • Verify all protective relays
  • Test emergency stops
  • Check interlocks
  • Validate trip settings

Calibration:

  • Current transformers (every 2-3 years)
  • Voltage sensors (annually)
  • Power meters (for revenue accuracy)

What We've Learned

The grid connection system is more than just wires:

Complexity:

  • Multiple voltage levels
  • Numerous protection systems
  • Precise monitoring required
  • Coordinated control necessary

Safety:

  • Multiple redundant protections
  • Fail-safe designs
  • Self-checking systems
  • Clear isolation methods

Efficiency:

  • Voltage transformation crucial
  • Minimizes transmission losses
  • Enables long-distance power delivery

Reliability:

  • Robust component selection
  • Comprehensive monitoring
  • Preventive maintenance
  • Fault tolerance

Understanding this system helps you appreciate:

  • Why certain faults cause immediate shutdown
  • How the turbine protects itself and the grid
