Operating Principle of a Wind Turbine with Squirrel Cage Generator Directly Connected to the Grid

The wind turbine simulated here uses a specific type of generator that determines much about how the whole system operates:

  • Generator type: Induction generator with short-circuited rotor (squirrel cage)
  • Connection: Stator directly connected to the Grid
  • No power electronics between generator and grid (simple, robust)

This combination creates both advantages and constraints. Let's understand both.

The Generator's Characteristic Curve

The key to understanding this turbine is this graph:

[Fig 1: Torque vs. Speed characteristic of an asynchronous induction machine]

What this graph shows:

Horizontal axis: Rotational speed (RPM) Vertical axis: Mechanical torque (Newton-meters)

The critical point: Synchronous Speed

There's a special speed marked on the graph called synchronous speed.

What is synchronous speed?

When the generator's stator is connected to the Grid (let's say 50 Hz):

  • The Grid creates a rotating magnetic field in the stator
  • This field rotates at a specific speed: the synchronous speed
  • Formula: Sync Speed = (Grid Frequency × 60) / Pole Pairs

For our turbine:

  • 4-pole (2 pole pairs): Sync = (50 Hz × 60) / 2 = 1,500 RPM
  • 6-pole (3 pole pairs): Sync = (50 Hz × 60) / 3 = 1,000 RPM

Two Operating Modes:

Look at the graph - the behavior is completely different on each side of synchronous speed:

Left side (below sync speed) - MOTOR mode:

  • Shaft speed < synchronous speed
  • Machine consumes electrical power from grid
  • Converts it to mechanical power
  • Torque is positive (driving the shaft)
  • Acts like an electric motor

Right side (above sync speed) - GENERATOR mode:

  • Shaft speed > synchronous speed
  • Machine produces electrical power to grid
  • Converts mechanical power (from wind) to electricity
  • Torque is negative (resisting the shaft rotation)
  • Acts like a generator

The transition:

At exactly synchronous speed:

  • No power conversion happening
  • Just spinning "empty"
  • Neither motor nor generator
  • Like neutral gear in a car

How This Affects Wind Turbine Operation

This characteristic creates important consequences:

1. Must reach sync speed before connecting

You cannot connect the generator to the grid until the shaft is rotating near synchronous speed:

Why?

  • If connected while stationary or slow → acts as motor
  • Would draw massive current from grid
  • Would try to accelerate to sync speed as a motor
  • Wrong direction of power flow!

Solution:

  • Wind spins the blades → spins the shaft
  • When shaft reaches ~1490 RPM (for 1500 sync):
    • Close enough to sync speed
    • Ready to connect as generator
  • Use soft-starter (thyristors) for gentle connection

2. Operating speed is nearly constant

Once connected as a generator:

  • Must stay above synchronous speed
  • But only slightly above!
  • Typical: 1,500 - 1,520 RPM (for 1500 sync)
  • Very narrow operating range!

Why so narrow?

Look at the curve near sync speed:

  • Small change in speed → big change in torque
  • Steep slope = sensitive response
  • This is actually good for stable operation

3. "Slip" determines power output

Slip is the difference between actual speed and sync speed:

Formula: Slip (s) = (Actual Speed - Sync Speed) / Sync Speed

Or: Slip (s) = (Actual Speed / Sync Speed) - 1

Example:

  • Sync speed: 1,500 RPM
  • Actual speed: 1,510 RPM
  • Slip: (1,510 - 1,500) / 1,500 = +0.0067 or +0.67%

The relationship:

  • Larger slip → more power generated
  • More power → more load on shaft → shaft tries to slow down
  • Wind torque balances electrical torque
  • Equilibrium reached at specific slip

At rated power (1,300 kW):

  • Typical slip: 0.5% to 1.5%
  • Speed: 1,507 - 1,520 RPM (for 1500 sync)

This is why these turbines are called "fixed speed" - the operating speed barely changes!

The Connection Challenge

Connecting this generator to the grid is delicate. Let's understand why:

The Problem:

Before connection:

  • Generator stator disconnected
  • No magnetic field in air gap
  • Shaft spinning from wind (say 1,490 RPM)
  • No electrical power being generated

After connection:

  • Generator stator connected to grid
  • Grid creates magnetic field immediately
  • Shaft must be at right speed and phase
  • Power starts flowing

If we connect wrong:

Too slow (way below sync):

  • Acts as motor initially
  • Draws huge current from grid
  • Creates shock torque on drivetrain
  • Could damage gearbox or coupling

Wrong phase:

  • Even at right speed, phases might not align
  • Creates transient current surge
  • Mechanical shock to drivetrain
  • Bad for equipment

The Solution: Soft-Starter

This is why we use the thyristor soft-starter! (covered in detail in Soft Starter section)

The process:

  1. Get shaft speed close to sync (1,490 - 1,495 RPM)
  2. Start thyristors firing at small angle
  3. Small current flows → small magnetic field
  4. Gentle torque applied to shaft
  5. Gradually increase firing angle
  6. Shaft accelerates smoothly to just above sync
  7. Phases align naturally
  8. Full conduction reached → connected!
  9. Switch to bypass contactors

All in 3-5 seconds, smoothly and safely.

Why This Generator Type?

Given all these complications, why use it?

Advantages:

✓ Extremely robust

  • Squirrel cage rotor: no brushes, no slip rings
  • No electrical connections to rotor
  • Very few things to fail
  • Can last 20+ years with minimal maintenance

✓ Simple

  • Direct grid connection
  • No power electronics needed (except soft-starter)
  • Lower cost
  • Proven technology

✓ Reliable

  • Fewer components = fewer failures
  • Well-understood maintenance
  • Parts readily available

✓ Grid support

  • Provides "inertia" to grid (mechanical mass)
  • Helps stabilize grid frequency
  • Unlike power-electronic systems

Disadvantages:

✗ Fixed speed operation

  • Can't optimize for varying wind speeds
  • Less efficient at winds below rated
  • That's why we have two-speed capability!

✗ Reactive power consumption

  • Needs reactive power from grid
  • Requires power factor correction
  • Capacitor banks often needed

✗ Complex connection procedure

  • Soft-starter required
  • Careful speed matching needed
  • Can't just flip a switch

✗ Limited controllability

  • Can't control power electronically
  • Must use blade pitch for power control
  • Slower response to changes

The Trade-off:

This technology dominated wind energy from 1980s - early 2000s because:

  • Advantages outweighed disadvantages
  • Cost-effective for the time
  • Reliable in harsh environments

Modern turbines use:

  • Variable-speed generators
  • Full power converters
  • Better efficiency at all wind speeds
  • But more complex and expensive

For learning fundamentals, this fixed-speed type is perfect - simpler to understand!

Introduction to Induction Machines

Let's dive deeper into how these generators actually work.

The Solar System Analogy

Before we get technical, imagine this:

Reference to video: "Magnets on opposing shafts"

The setup:

  • Two coaxial shafts (same centerline)
  • Each shaft has a magnet
  • Shafts can rotate independently
  • Magnets face each other with small gap

What happens:

If left shaft is locked (can't rotate):

  • Turn right shaft with the crank
  • Its magnet rotates
  • Left shaft's magnet tries to follow (magnetic attraction)
  • But it's locked, so it just creates torque
  • The angle between magnets indicates the torque

If left shaft has friction (like brake):

  • Turn right shaft
  • Left shaft rotates too, but slower than right
  • There's "slip" between the two
  • Angle between magnets depends on friction
  • More friction → larger angle → more torque

If left shaft is free (no load):

  • Turn right shaft
  • Left shaft tries to keep up
  • Eventually both spin at same speed (synchronized!)
  • No angle between them (perfectly aligned)
  • No torque transmitted (no friction to overcome)

This is exactly how an induction machine works!

  • Right shaft = stator (creates rotating magnetic field)
  • Left shaft = rotor (tries to follow that field)
  • Friction/load = electrical load on generator
  • Angle between magnets = "slip"

Creating the Rotating Magnetic Field (Stator)

How do we create that "rotating magnet" in the stator?

The Magic of Three-Phase Power

The stator has three sets of coils, connected to the three-phase grid:

[Fig. 2: Animation showing three-phase stator winding and rotating field]

What's happening:

The three phases:

  • Phase 1 (Red): Sin wave at 0°
  • Phase 2 (Green): Sin wave at 120°
  • Phase 3 (Blue): Sin wave at 240°

All at same frequency (50 Hz), but shifted in time

The stator coils:

  • Red coils: Connected to Phase 1
  • Green coils: Connected to Phase 2
  • Blue coils: Connected to Phase 3
  • Arranged around the stator circle

The resulting magnetic field:

  • Each coil creates magnetic field proportional to its current
  • Three fields (red, green, blue) sum together
  • Result: One magnetic field that ROTATES!
  • Rotates at synchronous speed
  • Smooth, continuous rotation
  • No moving parts in stator!

Why this is brilliant:

  • Electrical rotation (no mechanical rotation needed)
  • Very smooth field
  • Easy to control (just connect to grid)
  • This is one "magnet" from our earlier analogy

Creating the Second Magnetic Field (Rotor)

Now we need the second "magnet" - in the rotor.

Squirrel Cage Construction:

[Images showing squirrel cage rotor construction]

What it looks like:

The rotor is wonderfully simple:

  • Bars: Copper or aluminum bars running lengthwise
  • End rings: Short-circuit all bars together at both ends
  • No insulation: Bars are electrically connected
  • Laminated core: Iron laminations for magnetic path

Why "squirrel cage"?

If you look at just the bars and end rings (without the iron):

  • Looks like an exercise wheel for a pet hamster/squirrel
  • Bars = the rungs
  • End rings = the circular parts
  • Hence the name!

How it works - The magic of induction:

Remember Faraday's Law of Induction? "A changing magnetic field induces voltage in a conductor"

Here's what happens:

  1. Stator creates rotating magnetic field
    • Spinning at sync speed (1,500 RPM)
  2. Rotor is spinning slower (say 1,490 RPM)
    • There's 10 RPM difference (slip)
  3. From rotor's perspective:
    • The stator field is "sweeping past" at 10 RPM
    • This is a CHANGING magnetic field
    • Induces voltage in the rotor bars
  4. Rotor bars are short-circuited:
    • Induced voltage → current flows
    • Current flows through the bars and end rings
  5. Current creates magnetic field:
    • Current in rotor bars creates second magnetic field
    • This is our second "magnet"!
  6. Two fields interact:
    • Stator field (rotating at 1,500 RPM)
    • Rotor field (rotating at 1,490 RPM in space, but fixed to rotor)
    • They attract each other
    • Creates torque!

The beauty:

  • No electrical connections to rotor needed!
  • No brushes or slip rings
  • No switching or commutation
  • Everything happens through electromagnetic induction
  • Incredibly reliable

Motor vs. Generator Mode

Let's understand both modes:

Motor Mode (Below Sync Speed):

Example: Rotor at 1,400 RPM, sync at 1,500 RPM

  1. Stator field rotating at 1,500 RPM
  2. Rotor rotating at 1,400 RPM
  3. Relative motion: 100 RPM (stator faster)
  4. Large slip = large induced current
  5. Strong rotor magnetic field created
  6. Fields interact → torque
  7. Torque accelerates rotor toward sync speed
  8. Electrical power from grid → mechanical power

At Synchronous Speed:

Rotor at exactly 1,500 RPM

  1. Stator field: 1,500 RPM
  2. Rotor: 1,500 RPM
  3. Relative motion: 0 RPM
  4. No changing magnetic field
  5. No induced current
  6. No rotor magnetic field
  7. No torque
  8. Just spinning freely (like coasting)

Generator Mode (Above Sync Speed):

Example: Rotor at 1,510 RPM, sync at 1,500 RPM

  1. Stator field rotating at 1,500 RPM
  2. Rotor rotating at 1,510 RPM (pushed by wind)
  3. Relative motion: 10 RPM (rotor faster)
  4. Small slip = induced current (but reversed direction!)
  5. Rotor magnetic field created (opposite polarity)
  6. Fields interact → torque opposing rotation
  7. Mechanical power from wind → electrical power to grid

The direction of energy flow:

  • Below sync: Grid → Generator → Mechanical shaft (motor)
  • Above sync: Mechanical shaft → Generator → Grid (generator)

The slip determines power:

  • Small slip (1,502 RPM): Small power
  • Medium slip (1,510 RPM): Medium power
  • Large slip (1,520 RPM): Large power
  • Too large slip (>1,530 RPM): Might damage generator!

The nearly-linear region:

Near synchronous speed, there's a nearly-linear relationship:

  • Torque ≈ k × Slip
  • Where k is a constant

This makes control relatively simple:

  • More wind → more torque → slight speed increase → more slip → more power
  • Less wind → less torque → slight speed decrease → less slip → less power
  • System self-regulates to some degree!

Practical Operating Point

Let's look at real numbers for our turbine:

Large Generator (4-pole, 1500 RPM sync):

At rated power (1,300 kW):

  • Synchronous speed: 1,500 RPM
  • Actual operating speed: ~1,510 RPM
  • Slip: (1,510 - 1,500) / 1,500 = 0.67%
  • Very close to sync!

At partial power (500 kW):

  • Actual speed: ~1,503 RPM
  • Slip: 0.2%
  • Even closer!

Small Generator (6-pole, 1000 RPM sync):

At rated power (350 kW):

  • Synchronous speed: 1,000 RPM
  • Actual operating speed: ~1,008 RPM
  • Slip: 0.8%

Key insight:

The operating speed range is tiny:

  • 1,500 - 1,520 RPM for Large Generator
  • Only 20 RPM variation across entire power range!
  • That's why it's called "fixed speed"

Blade speed consequence:

Generator speed × Gearbox ratio = Blade speed

For Large Generator:

  • 1,510 RPM ÷ 77.73 = 19.4 RPM (blades)

For Small Generator:

  • 1,008 RPM ÷ 77.73 = 13.0 RPM (blades)

The blades are essentially fixed-speed too!

  • Large: 19-20 RPM
  • Small: 13-13.5 RPM

This is the fundamental limitation we work around with:

  • Two-speed operation (better wind range)
  • Blade pitch control (adjust power extraction)

Comparison: Squirrel Cage vs. Wound Rotor

For completeness, let's see the two types:

Squirrel Cage (What we have):

[Images of squirrel cage rotor construction]

Construction:

  • Copper/aluminum bars
  • Short-circuited by end rings
  • Simple, robust
  • No electrical connections

Characteristics:

  • Fixed characteristics (can't adjust)
  • Low maintenance
  • Very reliable
  • Lower cost

Wound Rotor (Alternative design):

[Images of wound rotor with slip rings]

Construction:

  • Actual wire windings on rotor
  • Three-phase winding
  • Connected via slip rings (rotating contacts)
  • Brushes transfer power to/from rotor

Characteristics:

  • Can access rotor circuit
  • Can add external resistance
  • Can control slip characteristics
  • Used in some wind turbines (different type)

Why we use squirrel cage here:

For the turbine we're simulating:

  • ✓ Simplicity preferred
  • ✓ Reliability critical
  • ✓ Lower maintenance
  • ✓ Proven technology
  • ✗ Accept fixed-speed limitation
  • ✗ Work around it with two-speed and pitch control
