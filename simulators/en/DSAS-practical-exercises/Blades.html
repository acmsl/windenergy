Why Do Blades Rotate?

Now for one of the most fascinating parts - the physics of how wind makes the blades spin!

The basic principles that govern the forces on wind turbine blades are the same ones that allow airplanes to fly. The rotation happens because the Hub has only one degree of freedom: it can only rotate around its axis.

Let's understand these forces step by step.

What Are These Forces?

Imagine we have an airplane wing in a wind tunnel, as shown in Figure 1. In a wind tunnel, the air flow is uniform and laminar (smooth, no turbulence), and we can control its speed.

[Fig 1: Airfoil in wind tunnel]

The Setup:

Inside the wind tunnel:

  • We place a wing (shown by its cross-section - the red outline)
  • It's held firmly from outside the tunnel
  • A bar passes through the wing's center (the circle)
  • This bar lets us rotate the wing
  • We can change the angle of attack (angle between wing and wind)

What the Wind Does:

When wind hits the wing:

  • Upper surface: Air speeds up → pressure decreases (Bernoulli's principle)
  • Lower surface: Air slows down → pressure increases
  • Result: Net upward force!

This creates a distributed pressure pattern (shown by the small dotted arrows):

  • High pressure pushing up from below
  • Low pressure pulling up from above
  • Forces all along the wing surface

Resultant Force:

Since the wing is a rigid body, we can represent all these distributed forces as:

  • One single force (the red vector)
  • Applied at a specific point (the center of pressure)
  • Magnitude and direction depend on:
    • Wing shape (airfoil profile)
    • Wind speed
    • Angle of attack (angle between chord line and wind direction)

Breaking Down the Force - Two Components

The red vector can be decomposed into two perpendicular forces:

1. Lift Force (F_lifting):

  • Perpendicular to the wind direction
  • Points "upward" (in airplane context)
  • This is what keeps airplanes in the air
  • The productive force!

2. Drag Force (F_dragging):

  • Parallel to the wind direction
  • Points in the direction wind is moving
  • Resists forward motion
  • This is what airplane engines must overcome
  • The parasitic force

The Key Ratio: Lift-to-Drag (L/D)

Good airfoils maximize this ratio:

  • High lift (useful force)
  • Low drag (wasted energy)
  • Modern wind turbine blades: L/D ratios of 100:1 or better!
  • Airplane wings: L/D ratios of 15:1 to 20:1

From Airplane Wing to Wind Turbine Blade

Now let's transform this to a wind turbine blade!

[Fig 2: Forces on a rotating blade, view from blade tip looking toward hub]

The Observer's Perspective:

Imagine you're standing at the tip of a blade looking toward the hub:

  • Wind coming straight at you (the blue vector)
  • Blade is rotating to your left (in the plane perpendicular to your view)
  • The black vector represents the rotation

The Two Wind Sources:

1. Real Wind (Blue vector - W_in):

  • The actual wind hitting the turbine
  • Coming from the left in the diagram
  • Parallel to the ground
  • Uniform along the entire blade length

2. Rotational Wind (Black vector - W_rot):

  • Created by the blade's own rotation
  • Perpendicular to real wind
  • NOT uniform along blade!

Why rotational wind varies:

Think about it:

  • Near the hub: blade moving slowly (small radius)
  • At the tip: blade moving very fast (large radius)

Formula: |W_rot| = 2 × π × Distance_from_Hub × Rotational_Speed

Example:

  • Blade length: 40 meters
  • Blade speed: 20 RPM
  • At hub (r = 0m): W_rot = 0 m/s
  • At mid-blade (r = 20m): W_rot = 2 × π × 20 × (20/60) = 41.9 m/s
  • At tip (r = 40m): W_rot = 2 × π × 40 × (20/60) = 83.8 m/s

Even with modest RPM, tip speeds are very high!

At 20 RPM with 40m blades:

  • Tip speed: 84 m/s = 302 km/h (188 mph)!

This is why blade speed is limited - tips approach sound barrier at high speeds!

The Total Wind (Resultant Wind)

3. Total Wind (Red vector - W_t):

  • Vector sum of real wind + rotational wind
  • This is what the blade actually "feels"
  • Direction changes along blade length
  • Magnitude changes along blade length

Vector Addition:

At any point along the blade:

  • W_t = W_in + W_rot
  • Use vector addition (not simple arithmetic)
  • Form a triangle with the two winds

Forces on the Rotating Blade

Now let's see what forces result:

The Total Force (Green vector):

Just like the airplane wing, the total wind creates:

  • Distributed pressure pattern on blade surface
  • Can be represented as single resultant force
  • Magnitude depends on:
    • Total wind speed (W_t)
    • Blade airfoil shape
    • Angle of attack

Decomposing for Wind Turbine:

For a wind turbine, we decompose differently than an airplane:

1. Tangential Force (useful!):

  • Component in the direction of rotation
  • This is what makes the blade spin!
  • Parallel to the rotation plane
  • Creates torque around hub
  • Gets transmitted to generator

2. Axial Force (Light brown vector):

  • Component along the hub axis
  • Points toward/away from hub
  • This is what tries to bend the tower!
  • Must be supported by tower structure
  • Creates bending moment on tower

The angle of attack matters hugely:

  • Optimal angle: Maximum tangential force
  • Too small: Not enough lift, low tangential force
  • Too large: Airfoil stalls, tangential force drops

Blade Twist - Compensating for Varying Wind

Remember: the total wind direction changes along the blade.

The Problem:

If blade had constant angle along its length:

  • At hub: good angle of attack
  • At tip: terrible angle of attack (or vice versa)
  • Can't optimize along entire blade!

The Solution: Blade Twist

[Fig 3: Blade twist illustration]

Each section along the blade has a slightly different angle relative to the rotation plane.

Why this works:

Near hub:

  • Real wind dominates (more perpendicular to rotation)
  • Blade section angled more into this wind
  • Achieves optimal angle of attack

At mid-blade:

  • Mixed wind direction
  • Blade section at intermediate angle
  • Still optimal angle of attack

At tip:

  • Rotational wind dominates (more parallel to rotation)
  • Blade section angled differently
  • Maintains optimal angle of attack

Typical twist:

Modern blades:

  • Root (near hub): 20-30° twist
  • Tip: 0-5° twist
  • Total twist along blade: 15-25°
  • Smooth transition (not sudden steps)

The Compromise:

Blade twist is fixed during manufacturing:

  • Optimized for a specific wind speed
  • Works well across a range
  • But not perfect at all wind speeds
  • Design point usually near rated wind speed

This is another reason why:

  • Variable-speed turbines are better (can adjust RPM)
  • Our fixed-speed turbine has two speeds (better match at different winds)

Let's put it all together:

Standing at the blade tip, looking at the hub:

You see:

  • Real wind coming from the left (blue)
  • Your motion through air creating wind from below (black)
  • The combination (red) coming at an angle
  • Blade oriented to meet this optimally
  • Forces created making the blade want to rotate

From the ground, watching the turbine:

You'd see:

  • Blades sweeping through vertical plane
  • Each point on blade experiencing different wind
  • Blade twisted to handle this variation
  • Smooth, efficient rotation
  • All from just air flowing past!

The Angle of Attack - Critical Parameter

Definition:

The angle between:

  • The blade's chord line (straight line from leading to trailing edge)
  • The total wind direction (W_t)

Why it's critical:

Too small (0-3°):

  • Not using airfoil efficiently
  • Low lift
  • Low tangential force
  • Wasted wind energy

Optimal (4-8° typical):

  • Maximum lift-to-drag ratio
  • Maximum tangential force
  • Efficient energy extraction
  • This is where we want to be!

Too large (>12-15°):

  • Airfoil begins to stall
  • Flow separates from upper surface
  • Lift decreases dramatically
  • Drag increases
  • Very inefficient!

Way too large (>20°):

  • Fully stalled
  • Turbulent flow
  • High drag, low lift
  • This is actually useful for stopping! (feathered position ~90°)

Controlling Power - Using Angle of Attack

This is where blade pitch control comes in!

By changing blade pitch, we change angle of attack:

Low wind - maximize power:

  • Pitch: 0° (blade aligned for optimal angle of attack)
  • Angle of attack: 6° (optimal)
  • High Cp (power coefficient)
  • Extract maximum available energy

Rated wind - limit power:

  • Wind increasing
  • Power approaching limit
  • Pitch: +3° to +6° (turning blade away from wind slightly)
  • Angle of attack: Still OK, but reducing
  • Lower Cp
  • Limit power to rated value

High wind - significant limitation:

  • Way more wind than needed
  • Pitch: +10° to +20°
  • Angle of attack: Getting large
  • Much lower Cp
  • Greatly limit power extraction

Extreme wind - stop:

  • Dangerous wind speeds
  • Pitch: 90° (feathered)
  • Blade edge-on to wind
  • Negative Cp (acting as brake!)
  • Extracts no energy, sheds wind safely

Why Blades Are Long

Now we can understand blade length!

Power Available in Wind:

The power in wind passing through an area: P = 0.5 × ρ × A × V³

Where:

  • ρ = air density (kg/m³)
  • A = swept area (m²)
  • V = wind speed (m/s)

The Area Term:

For a circular swept area: A = π × R²

Where R = blade radius

Doubling blade length:

  • Radius: R → 2R
  • Area: πR² → π(2R)² = 4πR²
  • Area increases 4×!
  • Power available increases 4×

Why not make them even longer?

Practical limits:

  • Manufacturing: Harder to make/transport
  • Strength: Must support their own weight
  • Tip speed: Already approaching limits
  • Cost: Materials and construction expensive
  • Tower: Must be taller (more expensive)
  • Grid connection: Power electronics must be larger

The sweet spot:

  • Small turbines (kW range): 2-5m blades
  • Medium turbines (100kW): 10-20m blades
  • Large turbines (1-2 MW): 30-45m blades
  • Huge turbines (5-10 MW): 60-90m blades
  • Offshore giants (15 MW): 100+ meter blades!

Our simulated turbine (1.3 MW):

  • Blade length: ~40 meters typical
  • Swept area: π × 40² = 5,027 m²
  • That's bigger than an American football field!

The Complete Picture - Putting It All Together

Let's trace energy flow:

1. Wind carries kinetic energy

  • Moving air mass
  • Energy proportional to V³
  • Distributed over swept area

2. Blades intercept wind

  • Air slows down (loses kinetic energy)
  • Pressure differential created
  • Forces on blade surface

3. Forces create torque

  • Tangential force × radius = torque
  • Torque rotates the hub
  • Kinetic energy → rotational mechanical energy

4. Hub drives drivetrain

  • Through rotor shaft
  • Through gearbox (speed increases)
  • To generator shaft

5. Generator converts to electricity

  • Mechanical rotation above sync speed
  • Creates slip
  • Induces current in rotor
  • Current creates magnetic field
  • Field interaction creates torque (opposes rotation)
  • Electrical power flows to grid

6. The balance:

  • Wind torque pushing blades
  • Electrical load torque resisting
  • System finds equilibrium
  • Steady-state operation

Efficiency cascade:

Not all energy makes it through:

  • Wind kinetic energy: 100%
  • Blade extraction (Cp): ~45% (theoretical max 59%)
  • Mechanical transmission: ~97% (gearbox, bearings)
  • Generator conversion: ~96%
  • Overall: ~40-43% of wind energy → electrical energy

That might seem low, but it's actually excellent!

  • Best coal plants: ~40%
  • Best gas turbines: ~60%
  • Photovoltaic solar: ~20%
  • Wind turbines: ~40% (and fuel is free!)

Summary - Why Blades Rotate

Let's recap the key concepts:

The Physics:

  1. Wind hits blade (real wind + rotational wind = total wind)
  2. Airfoil shape creates lift and drag forces
  3. Forces decompose into tangential (rotation) and axial (tower bending)
  4. Tangential force creates torque
  5. Torque rotates the hub and blades

The Optimization:

  • Blade twist compensates for varying wind along length
  • Pitch control adjusts angle of attack
  • Maintains optimal or controlled power extraction
  • Allows starting, stopping, and power limiting

The Energy Conversion:

  • Wind kinetic energy → blade forces
  • Blade forces → rotational torque
  • Rotational motion → electrical generation
  • About 40% efficiency overall

The Elegance:

  • No combustion
  • No pollution
  • No fuel cost
  • Just clever aerodynamics!

The blades are like giant, slowly rotating propellers working in reverse - instead of using power to create wind, they extract power from wind!
