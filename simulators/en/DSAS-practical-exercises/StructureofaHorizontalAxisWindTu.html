Structure of a Horizontal Axis Wind Turbine

A horizontal axis wind turbine (known as HAWT type) is an electrical energy production plant composed of subsystems located in four main areas: the Blades and Hub, the Nacelle, the Tower, and the Foundation.

PART 1: THE ROTATING ASSEMBLY - Blades and Hub

The Blades

  • Large airfoil-shaped structures fixed to the Hub
  • Designed with a special cross-sectional shape similar to airplane wings
  • Can be either:
    • Fixed pitch - cannot rotate along their longitudinal axis (older designs)
    • Variable pitch - can rotate around their longitudinal axis to control power

The Hub

  • The central component where all blades connect
  • Acts as the mechanical connection between the Blades and the Rotor Shaft
  • Mounted on a bearing system that allows only rotational movement
  • In variable pitch turbines, includes a platform that allows each blade to rotate around its own axis

How they work together: When wind strikes the blades, aerodynamic forces try to push them in various directions. Because the hub only allows rotational movement around the horizontal axis, all this force is converted into spinning motion—this is the first energy conversion from linear wind motion to rotational mechanical energy.

PART 2: THE NACELLE - The Power Conversion Center

The Nacelle is the large housing on top of the Tower that contains most of the wind turbine's machinery. It sits on a platform that can rotate horizontally to face the wind.

Inside the Nacelle - The DriveTrain:

These components form the mechanical power transmission system:

1. Rotor Bearing

  • Supports the entire weight of the Blades and Hub
  • Allows the Rotor Shaft to rotate smoothly

2. Rotor Shaft (Low-Speed Shaft)

  • Extends from the hub through the bearing
  • Rotates at the same speed as the blades (typically 10-40 rpm)
  • Connects to the gearbox input

3. Gearbox

  • A mechanical speed multiplier with fixed ratio
  • Why we need it: The blades rotate slowly (10-40 rpm) but electrical generators need to spin much faster (1000-2000 rpm)
  • Has two shafts:
    • Low-speed shaft (input) - connected to the Rotor Shaft
    • High-speed shaft (output) - connected to the Generator
  • Typical ratio: 1:50 to 1:120 (meaning the output spins 50-120 times faster than the input)

4. Flexible Coupling

  • Joins the gearbox's high-speed shaft to the Generator shaft
  • Purpose:
    • Compensates for small misalignments between the two shafts
    • Absorbs shock loads from sudden wind gusts or electrical changes
    • Acts as a mechanical torque limiter to protect the Generator

5. Electrical Generator

  • Converts rotational mechanical energy into electrical energy
  • In this simulator: a squirrel cage induction generator
  • Stator directly connected to the electrical Grid
  • Can be of various types in different turbine designs

6. Disc Brake

  • Usually located on the high-speed shaft (near the Generator)
  • Sometimes also on the low-speed shaft
  • Primary use: Lock the DriveTrain in place during maintenance
  • Secondary/Emergency use: Stop the DriveTrain if aerodynamic braking fails
  • Important: Not normally used for routine stopping—that's done with blade pitch control

Inside the Nacelle - Orientation and Control Systems:

Yaw System

  • Mechanism that rotates the entire Nacelle horizontally
  • Keeps the turbine facing into the wind
  • Consists of:
    • Yaw motors - electric motors that turn the nacelle (one set for each direction: clockwise and counterclockwise)
    • Yaw bearing - large bearing that allows smooth nacelle rotation
    • Yaw brake - locks the nacelle position when properly oriented

Wind Measurement Instruments

  • Anemometer - measures wind speed
  • Wind Vane - measures wind direction relative to nacelle orientation
  • Both mounted on top of the nacelle behind the blades

Inside the Nacelle - Support Systems:

Hydraulic System

  • Supplies pressure to the disc brake mechanism
  • Components:
    • Hydraulic pump
    • Oil reservoir
    • Valves for controlling brake application/release
    • Pressure sensors (pressostat)

Lubrication System

  • Keeps the gearbox properly lubricated
  • Includes:
    • Oil circulation pump
    • Filters and purification equipment
    • Heat exchanger to control oil temperature

Cooling Fans

  • Generator cooling fan
  • Gearbox lubrication system cooling fan

Temperature Sensors located throughout:

  • Generator stator windings
  • Gearbox oil
  • Rotor bearing
  • Ambient air in nacelle

Speed Sensors

  • Measure rotational speed on both gearbox shafts (low-speed and high-speed)
  • Critical for control and safety

Pressure Sensors

  • Monitor hydraulic brake circuit pressure

Optional Equipment (depending on model):

Capacitor Bank

  • Controls the power factor of electricity delivered to the grid
  • Improves electrical efficiency

Step-Up Transformer

  • Converts generator voltage (690V) to medium voltage (typically 15kV or 22kV)
  • Advantage: Reduces power losses in cables by lowering current
  • Can be located in the nacelle or at the tower base

PART 3: POWER CABLES

Power Cables carry electricity from the Generator to the grid connection point:

  • Exit the nacelle and descend through the tower
  • Hang freely for several meters to allow nacelle rotation
  • Then attach to the tower wall
  • Important limitation: Can only twist a limited number of times (typically ±2.5 turns) before the nacelle must untwist them

PART 4: THE TOWER

The Tower:

  • Supports the nacelle at the required height
  • Contains:
    • Power cables running down to the base
    • Vibration sensor - detects excessive tower movement
    • Access ladder or elevator (in larger turbines)
    • Electrical cabinet at the base

PART 5: FUNCTIONAL CONTROL STRUCTURE

From a functional perspective, the wind turbine has three main systems:

1. Physical Subsystems All mechanical, hydraulic, and electrical components described above

2. Electrical Power System

  • The Generator
  • Power electronic converters (if present)
  • Connection to the Grid
  • Transformers

3. The Controller - The "Brain" of the Wind Turbine

The Controller is the electronic system that makes everything work together safely and efficiently.

What makes the Controller special: Unlike most power plants that need human operators on-site, the wind turbine Controller enables fully autonomous operation.

How it works:

Information Gathering:

  • Continuously receives data from all sensors throughout the turbine
  • Wind speed and direction
  • Rotational speeds
  • Temperatures
  • Pressures
  • Vibrations
  • Electrical parameters (voltage, current, power)

Decision Making:

  • Analyzes all this information
  • Determines if conditions are safe for operation
  • Decides when to start, stop, or adjust operation

Action Taking:

  • Sends control signals to various subsystems:
    • Blade pitch angle adjustments
    • Generator connection/disconnection
    • Yaw motor activation
    • Brake system operation
    • Cooling system control

Autonomous Operation Example - Starting Up (Cutin):

When the Controller receives a "start production" command, it:

  1. Checks all conditions automatically:
    • Is wind speed in acceptable range?
    • Is the electrical Grid stable?
    • Are all temperatures normal?
    • Are all subsystems functioning properly?
    • Are vibrations within limits?
  2. If everything is OK, it executes the startup sequence:
    • Orients nacelle to wind direction
    • Releases brakes
    • Adjusts blade pitch to capture wind
    • Accelerates the drivetrain to proper speed
    • Synchronizes with the Grid
    • Connects the generator
    • Begins power production
  3. All without any external input or human intervention

Autonomous Operation Example - Shutting Down (Cutout):

When the Controller receives a "stop production" command (or detects unsafe conditions):

  1. Adjusts blade pitch to reduce power
  2. Disconnects generator from Grid in controlled manner
  3. Brings drivetrain to safe stop
  4. Applies brakes
  5. Records all event data

How Operators Communicate with the Controller:

The Controller receives commands through two channels:

1. Remote Communication (SCADA System)

  • SCADA = Supervisory Control And Data Acquisition
  • Computer system that monitors multiple turbines
  • Can be located:
    • In the wind farm control room
    • At a remote operations center
  • Allows operators to monitor and control many turbines simultaneously

2. Local Connection (Control Terminal)

  • Portable device carried by maintenance technicians
  • Connects directly to the Controller
  • Used for:
    • Detailed diagnostics
    • Manual control during maintenance
    • Viewing internal signals not available remotely
    • Changing operating parameters
    • Viewing alarm and event history

Safety Philosophy:

The Controller's autonomous design means:

  • ✓ Faster response to dangerous conditions
  • ✓ Consistent operation 24/7
  • ✓ No human error in routine operations
  • ✓ Can operate in remote locations
  • ✓ Always prioritizes safety over production
