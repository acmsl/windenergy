Functional Schematic of a Wind Turbine with Squirrel Cage Generator

Now that we've looked at all the individual components, let's see how they work together as a complete system. Think of this as putting all the puzzle pieces together.

Block Diagram - Functional Overview

Main Controller Functions

The Controller has two primary responsibilities in a squirrel cage generator wind turbine:

1. Generator Connection/Disconnection Control

Managing when and how the generator connects to the electrical grid.

What the Controller monitors:

Wind conditions:

  • Wind speed (from anemometer)
  • Is it in the acceptable range for operation?
  • Too low? Can't generate enough power
  • Too high? Dangerous, must shut down

Mechanical conditions:

  • Generator shaft speed (RPM)
  • Is it approaching synchronous speed?
  • Ready for grid connection?

Electrical grid conditions:

  • Voltage on all three phases
  • Are voltages within acceptable range?
  • Are all three phases present?
  • Is frequency stable (50 Hz or 60 Hz)?

What the Controller controls:

Thyristors:

  • Soft-start devices for gentle grid connection
  • Controller fires them at precise moments
  • Gradually increases conduction angle
  • Creates smooth acceleration to synchronous speed

Bypass Contactors:

  • Short-circuit the thyristors once at full speed
  • Reduces losses (thyristors have voltage drop)
  • Controller opens them for disconnection

Why this is complex:

Remember from the Soft Starter section:

  • Generator must be rotating near synchronous speed
  • Frequency must match grid frequency
  • Phase angle must align
  • All this must happen smoothly to avoid:
    • Mechanical shock to drivetrain
    • Electrical surge to grid
    • Equipment damage

2. Power Control from Wind

Managing how much power is extracted from the wind.

What the Controller monitors:

Just the wind:

  • Current wind speed
  • That's actually all it needs for this function!

What the Controller controls:

Blade pitch angle:

  • This is the magic lever for power control
  • Changes how efficiently blades extract energy
  • Remember the Cp (power coefficient) concept?

How pitch controls power:

Let's understand this clearly:

At low wind (below rated):

  • Want to capture maximum possible power
  • Pitch angle: ~0° (optimal Cp)
  • Extract as much as we can get

At rated wind (around 11-14 m/s):

  • Reaching generator's maximum power
  • Pitch starts to increase slightly
  • Beginning to "spill" excess wind

Above rated wind:

  • Way more power available than generator can handle
  • Pitch angle increases more (5°, 10°, 15°...)
  • Deliberately reducing Cp
  • Limiting power to protect equipment

In extreme wind:

  • Pitch to 90° (feathered position)
  • Cp becomes negative (blades act as brake)
  • Stops the turbine aerodynamically

The beauty of this:

One simple mechanism (blade pitch) handles:

  • ✓ Power optimization (low wind)
  • ✓ Power limiting (high wind)
  • ✓ Aerodynamic braking (stopping)
  • ✓ Emergency shutdown (extreme conditions)

3. Two-Speed Generator Connection Control

This is a special feature of the turbine we're simulating!

The challenge:

Remember the TSR (Tip Speed Ratio) concept?

  • For maximum efficiency, blade speed should match wind speed
  • But generator forces blade speed to be nearly constant
  • Single-speed operation is inefficient at low winds

The solution:

Use two different generator configurations:

  • Small Generator (6-pole, 1000 RPM sync speed)
  • Large Generator (4-pole, 1500 RPM sync speed)

When the Controller uses each:

Low to moderate wind (3.5 - 6.5 m/s):

  • Use Small Generator (6-pole)
  • Blade speed: ~13 RPM
  • Better TSR match for low wind
  • More efficient energy capture

Moderate to high wind (6.5 - 25 m/s):

  • Use Large Generator (4-pole)
  • Blade speed: ~19 RPM
  • Optimal for rated power production
  • This is where most energy is generated

The switching algorithm:

This is clever - let's walk through it:

Switching from Small to Large (wind increasing):

  1. Detect: Wind speed crosses 6.6 m/s threshold
  2. Wait: Confirm wind stays above threshold (avoid nuisance switching)
  3. Disconnect: Reduce power to zero using pitch control
  4. Open contactors: Disconnect Small Generator from grid
  5. Maintain pitch: Keep extracting just enough power to slow down gently
  6. Monitor speed: Wait for RPM to drop below ~1450 RPM
  7. Switch contactors: Change from 6-pole to 4-pole connection
  8. Cutin sequence: Start normal grid connection process for Large Generator
  9. Connect: When speed reaches ~1490 RPM, fire thyristors
  10. Complete: Full connection, back in production

Total time: Typically 20-30 seconds!

Switching from Large to Small (wind decreasing):

Similar process, but in reverse:

  1. Wind drops below 6.2 m/s (note: hysteresis - different threshold!)
  2. Cutout from Large Generator
  3. Let speed drop below ~950 RPM
  4. Switch to 6-pole configuration
  5. Cutin on Small Generator
  6. Back in production

Why hysteresis (different up/down thresholds)?

Without it:

  • Wind fluctuates around 6.5 m/s
  • Switches Large → Small → Large → Small
  • Constant switching!
  • Lost production time
  • Wear on contactors

With hysteresis:

  • Switch to Large at 6.6 m/s
  • Switch to Small at 6.2 m/s
  • 0.4 m/s "dead band"
  • Much more stable operation

Let's walk through the signal flow and understand how everything connects:

Left Side - Wind and Mechanical Systems

Wind Measurement:

  • Anemometer → measures wind speed
  • Signal goes to Controller
  • Controller uses this for nearly every decision!

Nacelle Orientation:

  • Wind Vane → measures misalignment
  • Signal goes to Controller.Yawing (yaw control subsystem)
  • Controller decides: need to yaw?
  • Commands Yaw Motors (CW or CCW)
  • Motors rotate nacelle
  • Process repeats (closed-loop control)

DriveTrain:

  • Blades capture wind energy
  • Transfer torque through Hub
  • Through Rotor Shaft
  • Through Gearbox (speed multiplication)
  • Through Flexible Coupling
  • To Generator

Speed Monitoring:

  • Generator Speed Sensor → measures high-speed shaft RPM
  • Critical for:
    • Knowing when to connect to grid (near synchronous speed)
    • Overspeed protection
    • State machine decisions

Pitch Control:

  • Controller calculates desired pitch
  • Sends command to Pitch Hydraulic System (or electric motors)
  • System adjusts Blade Pitch
  • Changes power extraction
  • Closed-loop: wind changes → pitch adjusts → power changes

Right Side - Electrical Systems

Generator to Grid Path:

Let's follow the power from generator to grid:

Generator output (three-phase AC, 690V) ↓ Power Cables (up through nacelle, down through tower) ↓ Electrical Panel at tower base:

Inside the panel:

  1. Busbars (connection bars)
  2. Current Transformers (measure current in each phase)
  3. Fuses (overcurrent protection)
  4. Thyristors (soft-start devices)
    • With Bypass Contactors in parallel
  5. More Busbars
  6. Generator Connection Contactors:
    • Small Generator Contactor (6-pole configuration)
    • Large Generator Contactor (4-pole configuration)
    • Only one can be closed at a time!

↓ Step-up Transformer (690V → 15kV or 22kV) ↓ Grid Connection (to wind farm substation)

Controller Monitoring Points:

The Controller watches:

Voltage measurements:

  • On each phase (Phase 1, 2, 3)
  • Checks for:
    • Proper voltage level (~690V)
    • All phases present
    • Balanced (no severe asymmetry)

Current measurements:

  • Through current transformers
  • Calculates:
    • Active power (kW)
    • Reactive power (kVAr)
    • Power factor
    • Per-phase current balance

Contactor status:

  • Each contactor reports: Open or Closed
  • Controller knows exact electrical configuration
  • Detects failures (contactor welded closed, won't open, etc.)

Thyristor temperature:

  • Bimetallic sensors
  • If any thyristor overheats → emergency shutdown
  • Prevents semiconductor failure

Controller Outputs - What It Commands

Let's see what the Controller actually controls:

1. Thyristor Gate Signals

  • Not just "on/off" - precise timing!
  • 6 separate signals (one per thyristor)
  • Firing angle determines conduction
  • Creates soft-start effect

2. Bypass Contactors

  • Command: Close or Open
  • When to close: After thyristors at full conduction
  • When to open: During cutout (disconnection)

3. Generator Connection Contactors

  • Small Generator Contactor: Close for 6-pole operation
  • Large Generator Contactor: Close for 4-pole operation
  • Interlocked: Can never both be closed simultaneously!

4. Blade Pitch Command

  • Target angle: 0° to 90°
  • Continuously adjusted based on:
    • Wind speed
    • Current power output
    • Operating state (startup, production, shutdown)

5. Yaw Motors

  • CW Motor: Activate for clockwise rotation
  • CCW Motor: Activate for counterclockwise rotation
  • Never both simultaneously!

6. Hydraulic Pump

  • On/Off command
  • Maintains brake system pressure
  • Controller logic:
    • Pressure low? → Start pump
    • Pressure high? → Stop pump

7. Brake Valves

  • Normal Brake Valve: Gradual pressure release
  • Hard Brake Valve: Rapid pressure release (emergency)
  • Controls how fast brake applies

Auxiliary Functions

Beyond the main control loops, the Controller manages:

Safety Monitoring:

Overspeed protection:

  • VCU (Velocity Centrifugal Unit): On generator shaft
  • Mechanical device trips at ~1803 RPM
  • Signals Controller: EMERGENCY STOP!
  • HCU (High-speed Centrifugal Unit): On rotor shaft
  • Mechanical backup if VCU fails
  • Trips at ~26 RPM blade speed
  • Last-resort protection

Temperature monitoring:

  • Generator windings
  • Gearbox oil
  • Rotor bearing
  • Ambient temperature
  • Any exceed limits? → Shutdown

Vibration monitoring:

  • Tower vibration sensor
  • Excessive shaking? → Something's wrong!
  • Could indicate:
    • Blade damage
    • Bearing failure
    • Foundation problem
    • Emergency shutdown needed

Grid monitoring:

  • Voltage on each phase
  • Phase loss? → Disconnect immediately
  • Under/over voltage? → Disconnect
  • Frequency deviation? → Disconnect

Hydraulic System Management:

Remember the brake hydraulic system?

The Controller manages it continuously:

Pressure monitoring:

  • Pressostat (pressure switch) tells Controller:
    • Above upper threshold? → Stop pump
    • Below lower threshold? → Start pump

Pump control logic:

  • Maintains pressure in "green zone"
  • Limits pump start frequency (avoid overheating)
  • Tracks pump run time (maintenance scheduling)

Brake release verification:

  • Brake Released Sensor (limit switch)
  • Confirms brake pads actually separated from disc
  • If sensor says "not released" but should be:
    • Problem! Hydraulic issue or sensor failure
    • Abort startup sequence

Nacelle Orientation Management:

Orientation tracking:

  • Controller maintains nacelle position in memory
  • Starting reference point established during installation
  • Every yaw movement updates position
  • Calculates cable twist count from position

Wind tracking:

  • Continuously compares:
    • Wind direction (from vane)
    • Nacelle orientation (from internal tracking)
  • Misalignment exceeds threshold?
  • → Activate yaw motors to correct

Cable twist management:

  • Monitors cumulative twist
  • Approaching limit (±2.5 turns)?
  • → Initiate untwisting procedure
  • → Temporarily disconnect from grid if needed
  • → Yaw in direction to reduce twist
  • → Reconnect when twist reduced

Information Flow Summary

Let's trace a complete control loop example:

Example: Wind Speed Increases

  1. Wind speed rises from 10 m/s to 12 m/s
  2. Anemometer detects change
    • Sends signal to Controller
    • Update rate: ~1 sample per second
  3. Controller calculates implications:
    • More power available from wind
    • Currently producing 850 kW
    • At 12 m/s, could produce 1200 kW
    • But generator rated for upto 1300 kW
    • Still OK to capture more power
  4. Controller maintains blade pitch:
    • Keep pitch near 0° (maximum Cp)
    • Let power increase naturally
  5. Generator speed increases slightly:
    • More torque from wind
    • Speed rises from 1508 to 1512 RPM
    • Still safe (well below overspeed limit)
  6. Power output rises to 1150 kW:
    • Controller monitors through current sensors
    • Calculates real-time power
    • Still below 1300 kW limit
  7. Wind continues rising to 14 m/s:
    • Now approaching generator limit!
    • Available power: ~1500 kW
    • Generator limit: 1300 kW
    • Must limit power extraction
  8. Controller increases pitch to 3°:
    • Reduces Cp from 0.48 to 0.42
    • Power extraction reduced
    • Power output stabilizes at 1290 kW
  9. Wind rises further to 16 m/s:
    • Controller increases pitch to 6°
    • Cp drops to 0.35
    • Power held at 1300 kW
  10. Process continues as wind changes:
    • Controller continuously adjusts pitch
    • Maintains power at or below rating
    • Protects generator from overload

All of this happens automatically, continuously, without human intervention!

State-Based Control

The Controller doesn't just respond to individual signals - it operates using State Machines.

Think of state machines as:

  • A flowchart of possible situations
  • Rules for moving between situations
  • Actions to take in each situation

Two main state machines:

1. Operation State Machine

  • Handles normal operation sequences:
    • Startup (cutin)
    • Production
    • Shutdown (cutout)
    • Generator switching
    • Cable untwisting

2. Security State Machine

  • Handles safety and fault conditions:
    • Normal operation monitoring
    • Fault detection
    • Emergency shutdown sequences
    • Recovery procedures

Why use state machines?

Without state machines (just reacting to sensors):

  • Complex logic becomes spaghetti code
  • Hard to predict behavior
  • Difficult to debug
  • Easy to miss edge cases

With state machines:

  • Clear definition of every possible situation
  • Explicit rules for transitions
  • Easy to visualize (can draw diagrams!)
  • Predictable, testable behavior

Example states:

Operation State Machine might include:

  • STOP - Not running, waiting for START command
  • Testing Wind - Checking if wind is suitable
  • Preparing for Cutin - Pre-startup checks
  • Waiting for Brake Release - Hydraulic system releasing brake
  • Accelerating - Blade pitch controlling acceleration
  • Thyristors Firing - Soft-start in progress
  • Production Small - Generating power with 6-pole configuration
  • Production Large - Generating power with 4-pole configuration
  • Switching Generators - Changing from Small to Large (or vice versa)
  • Shutting Down - Controlled cutout sequence
  • Untwisting Cables - Reducing cable twist count

Security State Machine might include:

  • Normal Operation - All systems healthy
  • Warning Level - Minor issue detected, monitoring
  • Alarm Level - Significant issue, taking protective action
  • Emergency Shutdown - Serious fault, stopping immediately
  • Waiting for Recovery - Fault cleared, waiting before restart
  • Awaiting Acknowledgment - Need operator to review fault

We'll explore these state machines in much more detail in the Practical Labs section, where you'll see them in action!

Integration - Everything Working Together

The beauty of this system is how all the subsystems coordinate:

Example Scenario: Morning Startup

Let's walk through a complete startup sequence and see all the systems working together:

6:00 AM - Night shift ending

  • Turbine has been stopped (wind was too low overnight)
  • Blade pitch: 90° (feathered)
  • Brakes: Applied
  • Generator: Disconnected from grid
  • Nacelle: Oriented approximately into last known wind direction

6:15 AM - Wind picks up

  • Wind speed rises to 4.2 m/s
  • Still below "Min for Cutin" (3.5 m/s threshold)
  • But above "Min for Yawing" (1.5 m/s)

Controller thinks: "Wind is strengthening. Let's prepare by orienting."

6:16 AM - Automatic yaw to wind

  • Controller checks cable twist: +0.8 turns (OK, within limits)
  • Checks wind vane: nacelle is 35° off
  • Activates CCW yaw motors
  • Nacelle rotates slowly (0.5°/second)
  • 70 seconds later: nacelle aligned (error < 5°)
  • Stops yaw motors
  • Applies yaw brake

6:30 AM - Wind continues rising

  • Wind speed now 3.8 m/s
  • Crosses "Min for Cutin" threshold (3.5 m/s)
  • Stays above for 30 seconds (avoiding false starts)

Controller thinks: "Wind is sufficient and stable. Ready to start!"

Operation State Machine enters "Testing Wind" state

6:31 AM - Pre-start checks

Controller runs through checklist:

✓ Wind speed in range? Yes (3.8 m/s) ✓ Grid voltage present? Yes (all three phases, 690V) ✓ No critical alarms? Yes (all clear) ✓ Hydraulic pressure adequate? Yes (85 bar, green zone) ✓ Generator temperature OK? Yes (ambient temperature) ✓ Cable twist acceptable? Yes (+0.8 turns, below 1.5 limit for cutin) ✓ All safety interlocks clear? Yes

Decision: Proceed with cutin!

State: → "Preparing for Cutin Small" (wind is 3.8 m/s, use 6-pole)

6:31:15 - Brake system test

Before releasing brakes, verify they work:

  1. Close brake valve (should already be closed)
  2. Command pitch to -5° (negative = into wind)
  3. Wait 5 seconds
  4. Check RPM: should still be 0 (brake holding)
  5. RPM = 0? ✓ Brake system OK!

State: → "Waiting for Brake Release"

6:31:25 - Release brakes

  1. Start hydraulic pump
  2. Monitor pressure rising
  3. Pressure reaches upper threshold (95 bar)
  4. Stop pump
  5. Monitor "Brake Released" sensor
  6. Sensor activates? ✓ Brake is released!

State: → "Accelerating to Sync Speed Small"

6:31:40 - Acceleration phase

Now the blades start turning:

Time 0:00 (6:31:40):

  • Blade pitch: -5° (capturing wind aggressively)
  • Rotor RPM: 2 RPM (just starting)
  • Generator RPM: 155 RPM (2 × gear ratio of 77.73)

Time 0:10:

  • Pitch: -3° (Controller moderating acceleration)
  • Rotor: 5 RPM
  • Generator: 388 RPM
  • Acceleration rate: ~23 RPM/second on generator

Time 0:30:

  • Pitch: 0° (optimal Cp)
  • Rotor: 10 RPM
  • Generator: 777 RPM
  • Getting closer!

Time 1:00:

  • Pitch: +2° (fine-tuning acceleration)
  • Rotor: 12.5 RPM
  • Generator: 971 RPM
  • Approaching synchronous speed (1000 RPM)

Time 1:20:

  • Rotor: 12.8 RPM
  • Generator: 995 RPM
  • Just below sync!

Controller thinks: "Close enough! Time to connect!"

State: → "Thyristors Firing Small"

6:33:00 - Soft-start sequence begins

Second 0:

  • Generator: 995 RPM (just below 1000 sync)
  • Fire thyristors: 30° conduction angle
  • Small current starts flowing
  • Generator sees weak magnetic field from grid

Second 1:

  • Increase to 60° conduction
  • Generator: 998 RPM
  • Torque from grid accelerating it

Second 2:

  • Increase to 90° conduction
  • Generator: 1000 RPM (synchronous!)
  • Phase locking with grid

Second 3:

  • Increase to 120° conduction
  • Generator: 1001 RPM (slight slip)
  • Power starting to flow TO grid

Second 4:

  • Increase to 150° conduction
  • Generator: 1003 RPM
  • Power output: 50 kW

Second 5:

  • Full conduction (180°)
  • Generator: 1005 RPM
  • Power output: 120 kW
  • Soft-start complete!

State: → "Production Small"

6:33:05 - Close bypass contactors

  • Thyristors at full conduction
  • Close bypass contactors (short-circuit thyristors)
  • Current shifts from thyristors to contactors
  • Turn off thyristor gate signals
  • Reduces losses (contactors have lower resistance)

6:33:10 - Normal production begins!

  • Wind: 3.8 m/s
  • Blade RPM: 13.0
  • Generator RPM: 1010
  • Power output: 145 kW
  • Power factor: 0.83
  • Generator: Small (6-pole)
  • Status: Producing!

All of this in about 2 minutes!

And it all happened automatically:

  • No human intervention
  • Controller managed every step
  • All subsystems coordinated perfectly
  • Safe, smooth startup

What We've Learned

This functional schematic shows us:

1. Integration complexity:

  • Dozens of sensors
  • Multiple control loops
  • Coordinated subsystems
  • All working simultaneously

2. Controller sophistication:

  • Not just simple if-then logic
  • State-based control for sequencing
  • Closed-loop feedback control for precision
  • Safety monitoring at all times

3. Autonomous operation:

  • No human in the loop needed
  • Handles normal operations independently
  • Responds to faults automatically
  • Only needs humans for maintenance and emergencies

4. Why simulation is valuable:

  • Too complex to learn from manuals alone
  • Too expensive to experiment on real turbines
  • Too dangerous to cause faults intentionally
  • Simulator lets you see inside safely!
