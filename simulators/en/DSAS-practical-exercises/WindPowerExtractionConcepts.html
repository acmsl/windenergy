Wind Power Extraction Concepts

Now that we understand why blades rotate, let's explore how much power they can extract and what factors control it.

This section introduces some of the most important concepts in wind energy: the power coefficient (Cp), tip speed ratio (TSR), and the theoretical limits of wind energy extraction.

The Fundamental Power Equation

Everything starts with this equation (referenced in the Bibliography):

P_extracted = 0.5 × ρ × A × V³ × Cp(V, Ω, Pitch)

Let's break down each term:

0.5: Just a constant from the kinetic energy equation

ρ (rho): Air density

  • Units: kg/m³
  • Standard value: 1.225 kg/m³ (at sea level, 15°C)
  • Varies with:
    • Altitude (lower at high altitude)
    • Temperature (lower when hot)
    • Humidity (slightly lower when humid)
    • Pressure (weather systems)

A: Swept area of the blades

  • A = π × R²
  • R = blade radius (meters)
  • This is why blade length matters so much!

V³: Wind speed cubed!

  • This is the most important term
  • Wind speed has HUGE effect
  • Double the wind → 8× the power!
  • Example:
    • 5 m/s wind: baseline
    • 10 m/s wind (2×): 8× more power!
    • 15 m/s wind (3×): 27× more power!

Cp: The power coefficient (or aerodynamic coefficient)

  • This is the mysterious and fascinating one
  • Let's explore it in detail...

The Power Coefficient (Cp) - The Heart of the Matter

What is Cp?

Cp is the fraction of available wind power that the blades actually extract:

  • Cp = 1.0 (100%): Perfect extraction - get all the wind's energy
  • Cp = 0.5 (50%): Get half the wind's energy
  • Cp = 0 (0%): Get nothing (blade at 90°, feathered)
  • Cp < 0 (negative): Blade acting as brake, putting energy INTO wind!

But Cp isn't constant!

It depends on three things:

  1. Wind speed (V)
  2. Blade rotational speed (Ω)
  3. Blade pitch angle

This makes it a complex, 3-dimensional function: Cp = f(V, Ω, Pitch)

Very difficult to visualize and understand!

Simplifying with TSR (Tip Speed Ratio)

Here's where engineers made a brilliant simplification.

The Problem:

Cp depends on both wind speed AND rotational speed separately. That's two variables!

The Insight:

What really matters is the ratio between:

  • How fast the blade tip is moving (linear speed)
  • How fast the wind is moving

Definition of TSR:

TSR = (Blade Tip Speed) / (Wind Speed)

More precisely:

TSR = (2 × π × R × Ω) / V

Where:

  • R = blade radius (meters)
  • Ω = rotational speed (revolutions per second, NOT RPM!)
  • V = wind speed (m/s)

Converting from RPM to rev/sec: Ω = RPM / 60

So the full formula becomes:

TSR = (2 × π × R × RPM/60) / V

Example Calculation:

Our turbine with Large Generator:

  • Blade radius: 40 meters
  • Blade speed: 19.4 RPM
  • Wind speed: 11 m/s

TSR = (2 × π × 40 × 19.4/60) / 11 TSR = (2 × 3.14159 × 40 × 0.323) / 11 TSR = 81.15 / 11 TSR = 7.4

Why TSR Matters

TSR tells us the relationship between blade motion and wind:

Low TSR (1-3):

  • Blades rotating slowly relative to wind
  • Like a paddle wheel
  • Lots of drag
  • Inefficient
  • Used in old farm windmills (pumping water)

Optimal TSR (6-9):

  • Sweet spot for modern turbines
  • Blade tip moving 6-9× faster than wind
  • Maximum efficiency
  • Our turbine operates here!

High TSR (10+):

  • Blades spinning very fast
  • Tip approaching supersonic speeds
  • Noise problems
  • Structural stress
  • Decreasing efficiency

The New Simplified Equation:

Now Cp can be expressed as: Cp = f(TSR, Pitch)

Just two variables instead of three! Much easier to visualize and optimize.

This means: the blades can extract up to 48% of the wind's kinetic energy under perfect conditions.

The Betz Limit - Theoretical Maximum

Wait - why only 48%? Why not 100%?

Albert Betz (1919) proved:

No wind turbine can ever extract more than 59.3% of wind's kinetic energy, no matter how perfectly designed!

Why this limit exists - The Logic:

Think about it:

If we extracted 100% of wind's energy:

  • Wind would stop completely at the blades
  • Air would pile up (nowhere to go!)
  • Would block more air from arriving
  • Turbine would stop working

If we extracted 0%:

  • Wind passes through unchanged
  • No energy captured
  • Useless!

The optimum is somewhere between:

  • Extract enough energy to be useful
  • But let enough air through to avoid blocking
  • Betz proved mathematically: optimum is 59.3%

The Betz Limit Formula:

Cp_max = 16/27 = 0.593

Real turbines achieve:

  • Old designs: Cp ≈ 0.30-0.40
  • Modern turbines: Cp ≈ 0.45-0.50
  • Best research designs: Cp ≈ 0.52
  • Theoretical limit: Cp = 0.593

Why we don't reach 59.3%:

Real-world losses:

  • ✗ Blade drag (not all force is useful)
  • ✗ Tip losses (air spills around blade tips)
  • ✗ Root losses (near hub is inefficient)
  • ✗ Non-optimal twist (compromise design)
  • ✗ Surface roughness (bugs, dirt, ice)
  • ✗ Wake rotation (wind spins after passing)

Our turbine at Cp = 0.48 is actually excellent!

  • 48% / 59.3% = 81% of theoretical maximum
  • Very good for a real-world machine

Operating Implications for Fixed-Speed Turbines

Now let's see how this affects our turbine.

The Challenge:

Remember, our generator forces nearly constant blade speed:

  • Large Generator: 19-20 RPM (1,500 RPM sync)
  • Small Generator: 13-13.5 RPM (1,000 RPM sync)

What happens as wind changes?

At low wind (5 m/s) with Large Generator:

  • Blade speed: 19.4 RPM (fixed by generator)
  • TSR = (2 × π × 40 × 19.4/60) / 5 = 16.2
  • Way too high! (optimal is ~7.5)
  • Looking at Cp surface: Cp ≈ 0.25
  • Very inefficient!

At rated wind (11 m/s) with Large Generator:

  • Blade speed: 19.4 RPM
  • TSR = (2 × π × 40 × 19.4/60) / 11 = 7.4
  • Perfect! (optimal is ~7.5)
  • Cp ≈ 0.48
  • Maximum efficiency!

At high wind (16 m/s) with Large Generator:

  • Blade speed: 19.4 RPM (still fixed!)
  • TSR = (2 × π × 40 × 19.4/60) / 16 = 5.1
  • Below optimal
  • But we use pitch control to reduce Cp anyway
  • To limit power to rated value

The Problem is clear:

Fixed speed means:

  • Only optimal at ONE wind speed (rated wind)
  • Inefficient at lower winds
  • Have to deliberately be inefficient at higher winds

The Two-Speed Solution:

This is why we have two generator speeds!

With Small Generator at low wind (5 m/s):

  • Blade speed: 13.0 RPM
  • TSR = (2 × π × 40 × 13.0/60) / 5 = 10.9
  • Still not optimal, but better than 16.2!
  • Cp ≈ 0.35 (vs. 0.25 with Large Generator)
  • 40% more power captured!

Switching strategy:

  • Wind 3.5 - 6.5 m/s: Use Small Generator (6-pole, 1000 RPM sync)
    • TSR closer to optimal
    • Better efficiency at low winds
  • Wind 6.5 - 25 m/s: Use Large Generator (4-pole, 1500 RPM sync)
    • TSR optimal near rated wind
    • Maximum power capability

The result:

This two-speed approach significantly improves energy capture over a single-speed design, even though it's not as good as true variable-speed turbines.

Variable Speed vs. Fixed Speed

Let's compare the approaches:

Variable-Speed Turbines:

How they work:

  • Generator not directly connected to grid
  • Power electronics (full converter) between generator and grid
  • Can control generator speed freely
  • Adjust speed to maintain optimal TSR as wind changes

Advantages:

  • ✓ Always at optimal Cp (except above rated wind)
  • ✓ 10-15% more energy capture annually
  • ✓ Reduced mechanical stress (can absorb gusts)
  • ✓ Better power quality

Disadvantages:

  • ✗ Expensive power electronics
  • ✗ More complex control
  • ✗ Electronics can fail
  • ✗ Harmonic distortion issues

Fixed-Speed Turbines (like ours):

How they work:

  • Generator directly connected to grid
  • Speed determined by grid frequency and pole count
  • Simple, robust

Advantages:

  • ✓ Simple, proven technology
  • ✓ Robust (fewer things to fail)
  • ✓ Lower cost
  • ✓ Provides grid support (inertia)

Disadvantages:

  • ✗ Only optimal at one wind speed
  • ✗ 10-15% less energy capture
  • ✗ Higher mechanical stress
  • ✗ Needs two speeds for decent performance

Two-Speed Fixed (like ours):

Middle ground:

  • Most of fixed-speed advantages
  • Captures 5-7% more energy than single-speed
  • Still simpler than variable-speed
  • Good compromise for 1980s-2000s technology

Modern trend:

Almost all new large turbines (>2 MW) are variable-speed:

  • Power electronics costs decreased
  • Reliability improved
  • Energy gains justify complexity
  • Grid codes often require it

But fixed-speed turbines still operate worldwide:

  • Proven reliability (20+ year track record)
  • Easy maintenance
  • Good learning platform!

Power Control Methods

Now let's see how we control the extracted power:

The Fundamental Challenge:

Available power from wind: P_available = 0.5 × ρ × A × V³ × Cp_max

At rated wind (11 m/s): P_available = 0.5 × 1.225 × 5,027 × 11³ × 0.48 ≈ 1,800 kW

But generator rated for only: 1,300 kW

Above rated wind, available power >> rated power:

  • At 15 m/s: ~3,900 kW available!
  • Must limit to 1,300 kW
  • How?

Method 1: Active Pitch Control ("Feathering" or "To-the-Feathers")

Principle:

  • Increase pitch angle
  • Turns blade edge-more toward wind
  • Reduces angle of attack
  • Reduces Cp
  • Limits power extraction

Control strategy:

Below rated wind:

  • Pitch = 0° (optimal Cp)
  • Capture all available power

At rated wind:

  • Pitch = 0° to 2°
  • Just reaching rated power

Above rated wind:

  • Pitch = 3° to 20° (depending on wind)
  • Deliberately reduce Cp
  • Maintain constant power output

[Fig 1: Pitch vs Wind speed for active pitch control]

Characteristics:

  • Smooth power control
  • Large pitch range needed (0° to 90°)
  • Relatively slow response
  • Most common method worldwide

Method 2: Passive Stall

Principle:

  • Blade designed to stall naturally above rated wind
  • Stall = airflow separates from blade surface
  • Lift decreases sharply
  • Drag increases
  • Power limited automatically

How it works:

Below rated wind:

  • Blade angle of attack optimal
  • Smooth airflow
  • Good Cp

Above rated wind:

  • Higher wind → higher angle of attack
  • Angle exceeds stall threshold (~12-15°)
  • Airflow becomes turbulent
  • Cp drops sharply
  • Power limited

[Fig 3: Power curve for passive stall turbines]

Characteristics:

  • Simple (no pitch control needed!)
  • Self-limiting (passive safety)
  • But:
    • Can't optimize for varying conditions
    • High loads on tower (large drag forces)
    • Less controllable
    • Mostly obsolete now

Method 3: Active Stall (Our Turbine!)

Principle:

  • Use pitch control to INDUCE stall
  • But pitch in opposite direction from feathering
  • Pitch INTO the wind (negative pitch)
  • Forces early stall

Why this works:

Stall occurs when angle of attack too large:

  • Can happen from wind increase (passive stall)
  • Can happen from pitching into wind (active stall)

Active stall advantage:

  • Smaller pitch range needed (0° to -10°)
  • Faster pitch response (smaller angles to move)
  • Still gives good control
  • Lower pitch mechanism loads

Control strategy:

Below rated wind (0 to 11 m/s):

  • Pitch = 0° (optimal Cp ≈ 0.48)
  • Maximize energy capture

At rated wind (11 m/s):

  • Pitch = 0°
  • Reaching rated power

Above rated wind (11+ m/s):

  • Pitch = -2° to -10° (negative = into wind)
  • Induces stall earlier than natural
  • Reduces Cp
  • Limits power

[Fig 4: Pitch vs Wind for active stall]

Notice the negative pitch values!

[Fig 5: Power curve for active stall turbines]

Advantages of Active Stall:

  • ✓ Smaller pitch actuators (less travel needed)
  • ✓ Faster response (quicker to change small angles)
  • ✓ Lower loads on pitch mechanism
  • ✓ Good control authority

Disadvantages:

  • ✗ Higher tower loads than feathering (more drag)
  • ✗ More complex controller logic
  • ✗ Less common (harder to find experienced technicians)

This is why our simulator uses Active Stall!

It's an interesting technology that was popular in the 1990s-2000s, especially for fixed-speed turbines.

Practical Operating Point Analysis

Let's see how all this plays out in practice:

Design Point Selection:

For our 1.3 MW turbine with 40m blades:

Rated wind chosen: 11 m/s

Why 11 m/s?

  • Site wind statistics (Weibull distribution)
  • This is where most energy production occurs
  • Not the most common wind speed
  • But the speed with most power!

Optimal TSR: 7.5

This determines blade speed at rated wind: Blade RPM = (TSR × V × 60) / (2 × π × R) = (7.5 × 11 × 60) / (2 × 3.14159 × 40) = 4,950 / 251.3 = 19.7 RPM

Perfect! Close to what Large Generator gives (19.4 RPM at 1,510 RPM generator).

Power at rated:

P = 0.5 × ρ × A × V³ × Cp = 0.5 × 1.225 × 5,027 × 11³ × 0.48 = 0.6125 × 5,027 × 1,331 × 0.48 ≈ 1,960 kW

Wait - that's more than 1,300 kW rated!

That's OK - we use pitch control:

  • Set pitch to +3° instead of 0°
  • Reduces Cp from 0.48 to about 0.35
  • P ≈ 1,960 × (0.35/0.48) ≈ 1,430 kW
  • Close to rated!

The generator and power electronics limit it to exactly 1,300 kW.

Annual Energy Production

How does Cp affect annual energy?

Simplistic calculation:

If Cp_avg = 0.40 (accounting for all losses and off-optimal operation):

Annual Energy = 0.5 × ρ × A × Cp_avg × ∫(V³ × hours) over year

For a good site (8 m/s average wind): ≈ 3,000 - 3,500 MWh per year

Capacity factor:

CF = Actual Energy / (Rated Power × 8,760 hours) = 3,250 MWh / (1,300 kW × 8,760 hours) = 3,250,000 kWh / 11,388,000 kWh = 28.5%

Typical for good onshore sites!

Offshore sites (higher, steadier winds):

  • Capacity factors: 35-50%
  • Much better economics

Key Takeaways - Wind Power Extraction

Let's summarize what we've learned:

The Power Equation:

  • Power ∝ Area (blade length²)
  • Power ∝ Wind speed³ (huge effect!)
  • Power ∝ Cp (efficiency)

The Power Coefficient (Cp):

  • Fraction of wind energy actually extracted
  • Depends on TSR and Pitch
  • Maximum theoretical: 59.3% (Betz limit)
  • Real maximum: 45-50% (our turbine: 48%)

Tip Speed Ratio (TSR):

  • Ratio of blade tip speed to wind speed
  • Optimal: 6-9 for modern turbines (ours: 7.5)
  • Fixed-speed turbines only optimal at one wind
  • Two speeds help (but variable speed is better)

Power Control:

  • Active Pitch (feathering): most common
  • Active Stall (our turbine): small pitch angles
  • Passive Stall: obsolete

Fixed vs. Variable Speed:

  • Fixed: simple, robust, less energy
  • Variable: complex, best energy capture
  • Two-speed fixed: good compromise

The Design Trade-offs:

  • Bigger blades = more energy (but more cost)
  • Higher TSR = more efficient (but noise, stress)
  • Better Cp = more energy (but diminishing returns)

All of these principles guide turbine design and operation!
