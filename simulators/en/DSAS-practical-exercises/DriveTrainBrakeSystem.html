DriveTrain Brake System

The DriveTrain, which includes the blades, has a brake mechanism that is NOT normally used to reduce the rotational speed of the blades. Instead, it serves as a mechanism to lock the DriveTrain in position once it has stopped (or is rotating at very low speed).

To reduce the blade rotation speed, the blade pitch angle is used: beyond a certain angle value, the blades push against the air, transferring kinetic energy from the DriveTrain to the air.

Brake Concepts in a Wind Turbine

The DriveTrain of a wind turbine can be in the following situations:

1. In Production

In this state:

  • The blade pitch angle allows wind energy to be captured
  • Energy appears as mechanical torque that drives the electrical generator shaft
  • Generator transforms it into electrical energy

2. Idle

In this state:

  • The blade pitch angle is positioned so it doesn't capture any wind energy
  • The DriveTrain doesn't rotate
  • This angle is 90 degrees - called "feather position"

Important characteristic of feather position:

  • The blade's aerodynamic coefficient is negative. Meaning: any rotation of the DriveTrain is braked by the blades. In other words: any rotational energy in the DriveTrain is used to "move the air". Therefore, the energy is transferred to the air and decreases
  • In this state, the DriveTrain may move slightly, typically oscillating around the nacelle's orientation. This position is called 'feathered position'

3. Braked

  • The DriveTrain can be in 'braked' mode when it's necessary to perform maintenance work on the wind turbine, or to ensure it remains stationary.


This state is guaranteed by a disc brake system (see Industrial Examples)

Functions of the Brake System

The brake system in a wind turbine has the following functions:

Function 1: Prevent DriveTrain Movement in "Parking" Position

Typically during maintenance operations, the brake:

  • Locks the rotor in a specific position
  • Prevents any accidental movement
  • Ensures worker safety

Function 2: Prevent DriveTrain Overspeed

The Controller continuously monitors the DriveTrain's rotational speed and activates a safety protocol to prevent it from exceeding a maximum value.

Two-Level Protection System:

Primary Protection - Aerodynamic Brake:

  • Uses blade pitch to create negative mechanical torque from the wind
  • Blades act as air brakes
  • Transfers DriveTrain energy to the air
  • Preferred method - no mechanical wear

Secondary Protection - Disc Brake:

  • If aerodynamic braking cannot reduce the DriveTrain speed within a specified time
  • Controller activates the disc brake system
  • This is an emergency measure

Critical Warning:

Stopping the DriveTrain involves dissipating the kinetic energy stored in it. Therefore, if the disc brake system is used, it can result in:

  • Significant disc deformation
  • Brake pad damage
  • Constitutes a major failure of the wind turbine
  • Requires inspection and likely replacement of brake components

Brake Location Options:

The brake disc can be located:

High-speed shaft (most common):

  • On the generator shaft
  • After the gearbox
  • Advantages:
    • More space available
    • Requires less braking torque (due to gearing ratio)
    • Easier access for maintenance
  • Disadvantage:
    • Only stops generator; gearbox still transmits some force

Low-speed shaft (less common):

  • On the rotor shaft
  • Directly connected to the Hub
  • Advantages:
    • Directly stops the entire DriveTrain
    • More positive control
  • Disadvantages:
    • Requires much higher braking torque
    • Needs much larger brake assembly
    • Less space available

Brake Actuation Equipment

The brake pad is pressed against the disc through a hydraulic circuit.

Fail-Safe Design Principle:

Most wind turbine brakes use a spring-applied, hydraulic-release design:

Normal Operation (Production):

  • Hydraulic pressure compresses powerful springs
  • Springs are held compressed
  • Brake pads pulled away from disc
  • DriveTrain free to rotate

Braking (or Power Failure):

  • Hydraulic pressure released (via valve or power loss)
  • Springs push brake pads against disc
  • DriveTrain stops

Why This Design?

  • Fail-safe: If hydraulic pressure is lost (power failure, hydraulic leak), brakes automatically apply
  • Safety first: System defaults to "safe" state (braked)
  • Industry standard for critical safety systems

Main Components:

  1. Hydraulic Pump
    • Builds pressure to release brake
    • Electrically driven
    • Controlled by Controller
  2. Accumulator
    • Stores hydraulic pressure
    • Provides emergency brake releases
    • Maintains steady pressure
  3. Pressure Relief Valve
    • Protects system from overpressure
    • Bleeds excess pressure to reservoir
  4. Control Valves
    • Normal brake valve (gradual release)
    • Emergency brake valve (rapid release)
    • Controlled by Controller signals
  5. Pressure Sensors (Pressostat)
    • Monitors hydraulic pressure
    • Signals to Controller
    • Has hysteresis (different on/off points)
  6. Oil Reservoir
    • Stores hydraulic fluid
    • Typically includes:
      • Level sensor
      • Temperature sensor
      • Filtration

Brake Control Logic:

The Controller manages the brake system to:

  • Maintain proper hydraulic pressure during operation
  • Release brake smoothly during startup
  • Apply brake when needed (normal stop or emergency)
  • Monitor system health (pressure, temperature, response time)

Monitoring and Diagnostics:

The system includes sensors to detect:

  • Brake released position (limit switch)
  • Hydraulic pressure level
  • Pump operation time
  • System response time
  • Failures or anomalies

All this information goes to the Controller for decision-making and fault detection.
