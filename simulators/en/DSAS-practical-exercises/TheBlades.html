The Blades - Detailed Design and Function

Wind turbine blades have similar requirements to airplane wings. The physical principles that make blades rotate are explained in the section [Why Do Blades Rotate?]. All the forces acting on a wind turbine blade can be resolved into two forces:

1. Lift Force (Lifting)

  • Similar to the force that keeps an airplane in the air
  • In a wind turbine, these are the forces that make the blades rotate

2. Drag Force (Dragging)

  • Opposes the air flow
  • In an airplane, must be overcome by engine thrust
  • In a wind turbine, must be supported by the Tower, which it tries to bend

Key Difference: Wind Turbine Blades vs. Airplane Wings

An important difference between the working conditions of wind turbine blades and airplane wings:

  • Airplane wing: Experiences uniform wind along its entire length
  • Wind turbine blade: Experiences non-uniform wind that varies along its length

The Two Wind Sources Acting on a Blade:

Figure 3 below shows the two wind sources reaching the blade:

1. Real Wind (Win)

  • The actual wind hitting the turbine
  • Indicated by the Win vector
  • Uniform along the entire blade length
  • Parallel to the ground

2. Rotational Wind (Wrot)

  • Created by the blade's own spinning motion
  • Proportional to:
    • The blade's rotational speed at any given moment
    • The distance from the hub center
  • Direction: Perpendicular to the real wind (Win)
  • Formula:

|Wrot| = 2 × π × DistanceFromSectionToHubCenter × RotationalSpeed

The faster the blade spins and the farther from the hub, the stronger this rotational wind

3. Total Wind (Wt)

  • The vector sum of both winds (Win + Wrot)
  • This is what the blade actually "feels"

Blade Twist - Compensating for Varying Wind

To achieve a uniform angle of attack (pitch) along the entire blade, each blade section is positioned at a slightly different angle relative to the previous section. This compensates for the changing ratio of real wind to rotational wind along the blade's length.

Design Compromise:

The optimal twist angle for each section depends on the wind magnitude (V). In practice, this requires a design compromise because:

  • Wind speed constantly changes
  • The blade twist is fixed during manufacturing
  • Designers must choose twist angles that work well across the expected wind range

How Blades are Manufactured

Wind turbine blades are complex composite structures. Here's an overview of the manufacturing process:

Materials:

  • Fiberglass or carbon fiber reinforced composites
  • Epoxy resin matrix
  • Balsa wood or foam core materials for structural stiffness
  • Gelcoat surface finish for weather protection

Manufacturing Steps:

  1. Mold Preparation
    • Large molds (one for each blade half - pressure side and suction side)
    • Molds are cleaned and prepared with release agent
  2. Layup Process
    • Layers of fiberglass or carbon fiber fabric are laid into the mold
    • Each layer is oriented in specific directions for strength
    • Core materials (balsa/foam) are positioned for structural rigidity
    • Resin is applied (either hand-laid or infused)
  3. Blade Halves Join
    • The two halves (pressure side and suction side) are bonded together
    • Structural components (spar caps, shear webs) provide strength
  4. Root Section
    • Heavy reinforcement where blade attaches to hub
    • Metal inserts for mounting bolts
  5. Finishing
    • Surface sanding and filling
    • Protective coating application
    • Lightning protection system installation
    • Balance testing

Quality Control:

  • Non-destructive testing (ultrasound, X-ray)
  • Load testing of samples
  • Balance and weight verification

Blade Pitch Control Mechanism

The blade pitch angle is used for two critical functions:

1. Power Control

  • Controls the power extracted from the wind and transmitted to the DriveTrain
  • During production, this power equals the power delivered to the Electrical Grid, minus losses from friction and other inefficiencies

2. Aerodynamic Stopping

  • Stops the DriveTrain aerodynamically
  • The disc brake system is only used to lock the DriveTrain once stopped, or as an emergency backup if aerodynamic stopping fails

Independent Pitch Control (Modern Turbines)

In modern wind turbines, each blade's angle can be controlled independently. The reasons for independent pitch control are:

1. Tower Shadow Effect

  • When a blade passes in front of the Tower, it experiences reduced wind
  • Independent control compensates for this temporary power loss

2. Wind Shear Compensation

  • Wind shear: Vertical variation in wind speed
  • Wind speed typically increases with height above ground
  • Depends on:
    • Turbine location
    • Terrain roughness
    • Nearby buildings
    • Wind direction
    • Time of day

Impact of Wind Shear:

In large wind turbines (long blades, tall towers), the difference in mechanical stress along the blade length is significant.

Power Quality Issue - Flicker:

If blade pitch doesn't compensate for tower shadow, the power output "flickers" each time a blade passes the tower. This creates:

  • Flicker in electrical power output
  • Impacts Power Quality delivered to the grid
  • Modern grid codes strictly limit this

Components:

Hydraulic Cylinder:

  • The piston is connected to the device that rotates the blade (see [Hydraulic Cylinder Pitch Control])

Three-Position Valve:

  • In the center position (as shown): Both cylinder ports are closed - maintains pressure on both sides of the piston (holds position)

Moving to the Right (Fig 2): When the valve moves right and the pump starts:

  • Oil flows to one side of the piston
  • Piston moves to the right
  • Blade pitch increases

[Fig 2 - Valve right position]

Moving to the Left (Fig 3): When the valve moves left and the pump starts:

  • Oil flows to the opposite side
  • Piston moves to the left
  • Blade pitch decreases

[Fig 3 - Valve left position]

Safety Feature - Fail-Safe Design:

Most systems include springs or accumulators that automatically drive the blades to "feather position" (90°, minimum wind resistance) if hydraulic pressure is lost. This ensures the turbine can be stopped even during power failure.

B. Electric Motor Pitch Control

In this system, blades are attached to a ring gear that meshes with electric motors:

Components:

Pitch Ring Gear:

  • Large gear ring on the blade root bearing
  • Each blade has its own gear ring

Electric Motors:

  • Multiple small motors (typically 3-4 per blade) drive pinion gears
  • Pinions mesh with the ring gear
  • Motors controlled by servo systems inside the Hub

Servo Control Systems:

  • Located inside the Hub
  • Respond to commands from the Controller
  • Control motor direction and speed
  • Monitor blade position continuously

Power Supply - Critical Safety Feature:

  • Fed by Uninterruptible Power Supplies (UPS)
  • Must guarantee enough energy to move blades to "feather position" at any time
  • Why this is critical: Even if the turbine loses grid connection, it must be able to stop the blades rotating
  • Battery backup typically sized for multiple full-range pitch movements

Advantages of Electric Pitch:

  • More precise control
  • Faster response time
  • Easier maintenance (no hydraulic leaks)
  • Better for cold climates (no hydraulic fluid freezing)

Disadvantages:

  • More complex electronics
  • Higher initial cost
  • Requires reliable battery backup

Historical Note: Aerodynamic Brakes on Fixed-Pitch Blades

In older wind turbines (pre-2000) and low power units (≤300kW), blades didn't have pitch control mechanisms.

Tip Brake Solution:

These models used an aerodynamic brake mechanism where the blade tip could rotate along the blade's longitudinal axis, as shown in the following figure:

[Image: Blade with aerodynamic tip brake]

How it worked:

  • Spring-loaded blade tip held in normal position during operation
  • When stopping needed, centrifugal force or mechanical release allowed tip to rotate
  • Rotated tip created massive drag, rapidly slowing the rotor
  • Simple, reliable, passive safety system

Video showing stopping with this blade type

Limitations:

  • Less precise control
  • Higher mechanical stress during braking
  • Not suitable for larger turbines
  • Phased out in favor of full-blade pitch control
