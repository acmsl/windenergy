Practical Lab 3: Generator Switching (Small ↔ Large)

Objective:

Understand how the turbine switches between the two generator configurations to optimize performance across different wind speeds. You'll learn:

  • When and why the Controller switches generators
  • The complete switching sequence step-by-step
  • Differences between switching up (Small → Large) and down (Large → Small)
  • How hysteresis prevents constant switching
  • What happens to power output during switching
  • Timing and production loss during the transition

Background - Why Two Speeds?

Before we begin, let's review why this turbine has two-speed capability:

The TSR Problem:

Remember TSR (Tip Speed Ratio) = (Blade Tip Speed) / (Wind Speed)

Optimal TSR: ~7.5 for maximum efficiency (Cp ≈ 0.48)

With only one speed:

At rated wind (11 m/s) with Large Generator (19.4 RPM blades):

  • TSR = (2π × 40 × 19.4/60) / 11 = 7.4 ✓ Perfect!

But at low wind (5 m/s) with Large Generator:

  • TSR = (2π × 40 × 19.4/60) / 5 = 16.2 ✗ Way too high!
  • Cp drops to ~0.25 (very inefficient)

The two-speed solution:

At low wind (5 m/s) with Small Generator (13.0 RPM blades):

  • TSR = (2π × 40 × 13.0/60) / 5 = 10.9
  • Still not optimal, but much better!
  • Cp ≈ 0.35 (40% improvement over Large Generator)

Summary:

  • Small Generator (6-pole, 1000 RPM sync): Better for winds 3.5 - 6.5 m/s
  • Large Generator (4-pole, 1500 RPM sync): Better for winds 6.5 - 25 m/s
  • Switching threshold: Around 6.5 m/s (with hysteresis)

Procedure Part A: Switching UP (Small → Large)

This happens when wind increases above the threshold.

Phase 1: Trigger the Switch

Step 1.1 - Increase Wind Speed

Gradually increase wind speed:

  • Current: 5.5 m/s
  • Slowly increase to: 6.7 m/s
  • Take about 10-20 seconds to increase (gradual)

Why gradual?

  • More realistic (wind doesn't jump instantly)
  • Lets you observe Controller's response
  • See the threshold behavior clearly

Step 1.2 - Observe Threshold Detection

The switching threshold: 6.6 m/s (from Small to Large)

Watch what happens as wind crosses 6.6 m/s:

At 6.5 m/s:

  • Still on Small Generator
  • Power increasing (more wind available)
  • No switching initiated yet

At 6.6 m/s:

  • Threshold crossed!
  • But Controller doesn't switch immediately...

Step 1.3 - Hysteresis Timer

The Controller waits to confirm wind is stable:

Why wait?

  • Wind might fluctuate right around 6.6 m/s
  • Don't want to switch constantly (oscillation)
  • Confirm wind is staying above threshold
  • Typical wait time: 15-30 seconds

What you see:

  • Wind stable at 6.7 m/s
  • Still producing on Small Generator
  • Power still flowing normally
  • Controller counting down internally (you might not see this)

After wait period expires:

  • Controller decides: "Wind is stable above threshold"
  • Decision: Switch to Large Generator
  • Switching sequence begins!

Record the time: When did switching sequence start?

Phase 2: Cutout from Small Generator

The switching process is essentially:

  1. Controlled shutdown (cutout) from Small Generator
  2. Controlled startup (cutin) on Large Generator

Step 2.1 - Power Reduction

Same as normal cutout (Lab 2):

Pitch increases:

  • From ~0° (optimal for current wind)
  • To ~40-50° (reducing power extraction)
  • Takes 20-30 seconds

Power decreases:

  • From ~400 kW (at 6.7 m/s with Small)
  • Gradually to ~5 kW
  • Smooth reduction

Generator speed:

  • Decreases from ~1012 RPM
  • Approaches 1000 RPM (sync speed for Small Generator)
  • Ready for disconnection

State Machine:

  • State changes to: "Switching to Large Generator" (or similar)
  • Indicates this is a planned transition, not a fault

Step 2.2 - Electrical Disconnection

When power near zero:

Sequence:

  1. Open bypass contactors
  2. Ensure thyristors off
  3. Open Small Generator Contactor
  4. Generator electrically isolated

Status:

  • "Small Generator Contactor: Open"
  • "Bypass Contactors: Open"
  • Power: 0 kW
  • Generator freewheeling at ~995 RPM

Note the difference:

  • In normal cutout: we'd apply brake now
  • In switching: we keep spinning!
  • Need that rotational energy for next phase

Step 2.3 - Speed Transition

Now the interesting part - changing rotational speed!

Current state:

  • Blades spinning at ~12.8 RPM (from Small Generator operation)
  • Generator at ~995 RPM
  • Need to reach ~1490 RPM for Large Generator connection

How to accelerate?

Use blade pitch control:

  • Reduce pitch from ~45° back toward 0°
  • Blades start extracting power again
  • Wind torque accelerates the drivetrain
  • But no electrical connection yet (freewheeling acceleration)

Analysis of Upward Switch

Total Time Breakdown:

Typical timing for Small → Large switch:

  1. Hysteresis wait: 15-30 seconds
  2. Power reduction (Small): 25-35 seconds
  3. Speed transition: 20-30 seconds
  4. Soft-start (Large): 5-7 seconds

Total: ~70-100 seconds (including hysteresis)

Production Loss:

During switching:

  • Zero power for ~30-40 seconds (disconnection + transition)
  • Some power before (reduction phase)
  • Some power after (building back up)

Energy lost:

  • Assuming could have produced 400 kW continuously
  • Lost production time: ~1 minute
  • Energy lost: ~7 kWh (400 kW × 1 min/60)

Is it worth it?

After switching at 6.7 m/s:

  • Small Generator: ~400 kW (TSR = 6.8, Cp ≈ 0.42)
  • Large Generator: ~480 kW (TSR = 10.1, Cp ≈ 0.48)
  • Gain: 80 kW (20% more power!)

Payback time:

  • Lost 7 kWh during switching
  • Gaining 80 kW continuously
  • Payback: 7 kWh / 80 kW = ~5 minutes

After just 5 minutes, the energy gain exceeds the switching loss!

If wind stays at 6.7+ m/s for hours, the benefit is huge.

Procedure Part B: Switching DOWN (Large → Small)

Now let's see the reverse: switching from Large to Small when wind decreases.

Phase 1: Setup - Large Generator Production

Current state from Part A:

  • Wind: 6.7 m/s
  • Generator: Large (4-pole)
  • Power: ~480 kW
  • Production: Normal

Phase 2: Trigger Downward Switch

Step 2.1 - Decrease Wind Speed

Gradually decrease wind:

  • From: 6.7 m/s
  • To: 6.0 m/s
  • Take 15-20 seconds (gradual)

Watch for threshold:

The downward switching threshold: 6.2 m/s

Notice: Different from upward threshold (6.6 m/s)!

This is hysteresis:

  • Switch up at: 6.6 m/s
  • Switch down at: 6.2 m/s
  • Dead band: 0.4 m/s

Why different thresholds?

Without hysteresis:

  • Wind fluctuates around 6.4 m/s
  • Crosses 6.4 m/s up and down constantly
  • Would switch constantly: Small → Large → Small → Large...
  • Lost production time
  • Wear on contactors
  • Very inefficient!

With hysteresis (dead band):

  • Wind at 6.4 m/s → stays on current generator
  • Must change significantly to trigger switch
  • Much more stable operation
  • Worth the slight sub-optimal performance in dead band

Step 2.2 - Cross Threshold

At 6.3 m/s:

  • Still on Large Generator
  • Power decreasing (less wind)
  • No switching yet

At 6.2 m/s:

  • Threshold crossed!
  • Controller starts hysteresis timer
  • Waits to confirm wind staying below 6.2

After wait period (~15-30 sec):

  • Wind confirmed stable at 6.0 m/s
  • Decision: Switch to Small Generator
  • Switching sequence begins

Record time: When did downward switch start?

Phase 3: Cutout from Large Generator

Step 3.1 - Power Reduction

Same process as upward switch:

Pitch increases:

  • From ~+2° (optimal for 6.0 m/s)
  • To ~45° (nearly feathered)
  • Power reduces to near zero
  • Takes 25-30 seconds

Generator speed:

  • Decreases from ~1507 RPM
  • Approaches 1500 RPM (Large Generator sync)

Step 3.2 - Electrical Disconnection

When power near zero:

Sequence:

  1. Open bypass contactors
  2. Thyristors off
  3. Open Large Generator Contactor
  4. Generator isolated

Status:

  • "Large Generator Contactor: Open"
  • Generator freewheeling at ~1495 RPM
  • Blades at ~19.2 RPM

Phase 4: Speed Transition (Deceleration)

Now the opposite of before - we need to SLOW DOWN!

Current state:

  • Blades: ~19.2 RPM (from Large Generator)
  • Generator: ~1495 RPM
  • Need: ~995 RPM (for Small Generator connection)
  • Must decelerate: ~500 RPM

How to decelerate?

Two methods possible:

Method 1: Passive aerodynamic braking

  • Keep pitch at high angle (45°+)
  • Cp negative (blades act as brake)
  • Slowly coast down
  • Takes a long time (~60 seconds)

Method 2: Active pitch control (used by most turbines)

  • Increase pitch to 70-90° (very high)
  • Strong aerodynamic braking
  • Faster deceleration
  • More controlled

Our turbine likely uses Method 2:

Analysis of Downward Switch

Total Time:

Similar to upward switch:

  1. Hysteresis wait: 15-30 seconds
  2. Power reduction (Large): 25-35 seconds
  3. Speed transition (deceleration): 20-30 seconds
  4. Soft-start (Small): 5-7 seconds

Total: ~70-100 seconds

Production Loss:

Was it worth switching down?

Payback:

  • Lost 7 kWh during switching
  • Gaining 40 kW continuously
  • Payback: 7 kWh / 40 kW = ~10 minutes

Still worth it if wind stays at 6.0 m/s for a while!

Observations and Questions

Things to Notice:

1. The hysteresis behavior:

Try this experiment:

  • Start on Small Generator at 6.4 m/s
  • Wind is in "dead band" (6.2 - 6.6)
  • What happens? → Stays on Small
  • Now start on Large Generator at 6.4 m/s
  • What happens? → Stays on Large
  • Same wind, different generator depending on history!

2. Production during switching:

Look at power output graph during switch:

  • Smooth decrease to zero
  • Zero for 30-40 seconds
  • Smooth increase back up
  • Creates a distinct "U" shape in the graph

3. Pitch behavior variation:

During acceleration (up-switch):

  • Pitch goes negative briefly (aggressive capture)
  • Then positive to control approach

During deceleration (down-switch):

  • Pitch goes very high (strong braking)
  • Then reduces to control approach

4. Contactors never both closed:

Critical safety interlock:

  • Small Generator Contactor closes/opens
  • Large Generator Contactor opens/closes
  • Never both closed simultaneously!
  • Would short-circuit generator windings → catastrophic!

Experiment 3: Measure Switching Energy Loss

Detailed measurement:

  1. Note power output before switching: P_before
  2. Press timer start when switching begins
  3. Integrate power during entire switching sequence
  4. Note power output after switching: P_after
  5. Calculate energy lost

Formula:

  • If could have continued at P_before
  • Duration: t_switch seconds
  • Energy that WOULD have been produced: E_potential = P_before × t_switch
  • Energy ACTUALLY produced: E_actual (integrate power curve)
  • Energy lost: E_lost = E_potential - E_actual

Typical result: ~6-8 kWh per switch

Common Issues and Troubleshooting

Issue 1: "Stuck in Switching State"

Symptom:

  • Switching sequence started
  • But hasn't completed after several minutes
  • State: "Switching to [Large/Small]"
  • Not producing power

Possible causes:

  • Can't reach target speed (wind changed)
  • Pitch system not responding
  • Speed sensor fault

Diagnosis:

  • Check current generator speed
  • Is it accelerating/decelerating toward target?
  • Check pitch angle - is it responding to commands?
  • Check wind speed - did it change significantly?

Solution:

  • If wind changed: may eventually reach target anyway
  • If pitch stuck: controller may abort and shutdown
  • If stuck completely: may need emergency stop

Issue 2: "Wrong Generator Selected on Startup"

Symptom:

  • Wind is 7 m/s (expect Large Generator)
  • But starts on Small Generator
  • Or vice versa

Possible causes:

  • Wind speed sensor reading incorrect
  • Recent history affecting decision
  • Control logic issue

Diagnosis:

  • Check actual vs. displayed wind speed
  • What was wind speed when START was pressed?
  • Controller decides at START time, not current time

Solution:

  • Usually self-corrects (will switch to proper generator)
  • If sensor fault: needs maintenance
  • If consistent wrong choice: controller configuration issue

Issue 3: "Excessive Switching Frequency"

Symptom:

  • Turbine switching every few minutes
  • Constantly switching back and forth
  • Lost production time significant

Possible causes:

  • Hysteresis not working correctly
  • Dead band too narrow
  • Wind genuinely extremely variable
  • Sensor noise creating false threshold crossings

Diagnosis:

  • Monitor actual wind speed carefully
  • Is it truly fluctuating across thresholds?
  • Plot wind speed vs. time
  • Check switching log - what triggers are recorded?

Solution:

  • If real wind fluctuation: normal (unfortunate) behavior
  • If sensor noise: filter or replace sensor
  • If hysteresis fault: controller parameter adjustment needed

Issue 4: "Switch Aborted - Timeout"

Symptom:

  • Switching started
  • Timeout error after 90-120 seconds
  • Returns to previous generator (or shuts down)

Possible causes:

  • Couldn't reach target speed in time
  • Wind changed during switching
  • Mechanical problem preventing speed change

Diagnosis:

  • Check wind speed during switching attempt
  • Did wind drop/increase significantly?
  • Check if speed was changing at all
  • Review incident log for details

Solution:

  • If wind changed: normal, will retry when wind stable
  • If mechanical: needs investigation
  • Controller protects itself by aborting incomplete switch

Advanced Topic: Is Two-Speed Still Optimal?

Historical context:

This technology was popular in 1990s-2000s because:

  • ✓ Better than single-speed (5-10% more energy)
  • ✓ Much simpler than variable-speed with converters
  • ✓ Power electronics were expensive and unreliable then
  • ✓ Good compromise for the technology of the time

Modern alternative: Variable-Speed

Today's large turbines use:

  • Full power converter (generator → AC → DC → AC → grid)
  • Can vary generator speed continuously
  • Always maintain optimal TSR
  • No switching discontinuity
  • 10-15% more energy than fixed-speed
  • But complex and expensive

Why study two-speed?

  3. Good learning platform (clear cause-effect)
  4. Historical significance (important evolution step)

Understanding two-speed helps you understand:
