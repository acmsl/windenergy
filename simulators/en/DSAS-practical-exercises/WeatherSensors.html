Weather Sensors

Why Weather Sensors Matter

A wind turbine's entire purpose is to capture wind energy - so knowing exactly what the wind is doing is absolutely critical. The Controller makes dozens of decisions every minute based on weather data:

  • When to start up (cutin)
  • When to shut down (cutout)
  • How to orient the nacelle
  • What blade pitch angle to use
  • Whether conditions are safe

Let's look at the sensors that provide this vital information.

Wind Sensors

Anemometers - Measuring Wind Speed

An anemometer measures how fast the wind is blowing. There are several types, each with advantages and disadvantages:

1. Cup Anemometer (The Classic)

This is the type you probably picture when you think "anemometer":

[Fig 1: Cup anemometer diagram]

How it works:

The design:

  • Three or four cup-shaped "scoops" mounted on arms
  • Arms attach to a vertical shaft
  • Wind pushes the cups, making them spin
  • Faster wind = faster spinning

The measurement:

  • A rotary encoder attached to the shaft generates electrical pulses
  • Number of pulses per rotation is known (typically 30-60)
  • Electronics count pulses per second
  • More pulses per second = higher wind speed
  • Formula converts pulse frequency to wind speed (m/s or mph)
  • Advantages:
  • ✓ Simple, robust design
  • ✓ Been around for 150+ years (proven technology)
  • ✓ Omnidirectional (works regardless of nacelle orientation)
  • ✓ Relatively inexpensive ($500-2,000)
  • ✓ Easy to maintain

Disadvantages:

  • ✗ Moving parts wear out (bearings)
  • ✗ Can freeze in ice/snow
  • ✗ Birds sometimes perch on it (affects readings!)
  • ✗ Slightly non-linear response (see calibration curve below)
  • Calibration curve:

The relationship between wind speed and pulse frequency is mostly linear, but not perfectly:

[Fig 2: Calibration curve showing wind speed vs. output frequency]

Notice the slight curve - at very low speeds, friction affects accuracy. Modern controllers compensate for this using the calibration data.

Typical specifications:

  • Range: 0-60 m/s (0-134 mph)
  • Accuracy: ±0.3 m/s or ±2% (whichever is greater)
  • Starting threshold: ~0.5 m/s (below this, friction prevents rotation)
  • Response time: ~1 second
  • Lifespan: 5-10 years (bearing-dependent)
  • 2. Ultrasonic Anemometer

Modern turbines increasingly use ultrasonic anemometers - no moving parts!

[Diagram of ultrasonic anemometer]

How it works:

The clever physics:

Imagine two people throwing a ball back and forth:

  • In still air: ball takes same time in both directions
  • With wind: ball travels faster downwind, slower upwind
  • By measuring the time difference, you can calculate wind speed

Ultrasonic anemometers do exactly this, but with sound waves:

The setup:

  • Pairs of ultrasonic transducers (both transmitter and receiver)
  • Typically 2-3 pairs at different angles
  • Each pair separated by known distance (10-20 cm)

The measurement:

  1. Transducer A sends ultrasonic pulse to Transducer B
  2. Measure time for pulse to travel (affected by wind)
  3. Transducer B sends pulse back to Transducer A
  4. Measure return time (affected by wind in opposite direction)
  5. Calculate wind speed from time difference
  6. Repeat thousands of times per second
  7. Use multiple pairs to determine both speed AND direction
  8. The math (simplified):
  • If wind is blowing from A to B:
    • Time A→B is shorter (sound travels faster with wind)
    • Time B→A is longer (sound travels slower against wind)
  • Wind speed is proportional to the time difference

Advantages:

  • ✓ No moving parts (nothing to wear out!)
  • ✓ Works in ice and snow
  • ✓ Measures wind speed AND direction simultaneously
  • ✓ Very fast response time (<0.1 second)
  • ✓ Accurate even in turbulent wind
  • ✓ Long lifespan (15-20 years)
  • ✓ Low maintenance

Disadvantages:

  • ✗ More expensive ($3,000-8,000)
  • ✗ Can be affected by rain/snow droplets (though modern ones compensate)
  • ✗ Requires electronics (can fail, though rarely)

Why they're becoming standard:

The reliability and accuracy advantages outweigh the higher initial cost. Many new turbines use ultrasonic anemometers exclusively.

3. Acoustic Resonance Anemometer

This is a specialized ultrasonic type that's particularly clever:

How it works:

Standing wave principle:

  • Creates a chamber with ultrasonic standing waves
  • Standing waves have fixed patterns in still air
  • Wind deforms these wave patterns
  • Measures the phase shift of the standing waves
  • Phase shift is proportional to wind speed and direction

Think of it like:

  • Blowing across the top of a bottle changes the sound pitch
  • This uses the same physics but measures it precisely

Advantages:

  • ✓ Extremely robust and reliable
  • ✓ Very accurate
  • ✓ No moving parts
  • ✓ Works in harsh conditions

Disadvantages:

  • ✗ Expensive ($5,000-10,000)
  • ✗ Complex electronics

Typical use:

  • High-value turbines where maximum reliability needed
  • Offshore installations (harsh conditions, difficult access)
  • Met masts (meteorological measurement towers)

4. LIDAR (Light Detection and Ranging)

This is cutting-edge technology - using lasers to measure wind!

How it works:

The Doppler effect:

You know how a car's engine sound changes as it passes you? That's the Doppler effect - frequency changes when source is moving.

LIDAR uses this principle:

  1. Emit laser pulse forward from the turbine
  2. Laser hits particles in the air (dust, water droplets, aerosols)
  3. Particles moving with the wind reflect the laser back
  4. Reflected laser has frequency shift (Doppler effect)
  5. Frequency shift reveals wind speed
  6. Laser can be aimed at different distances (100-500m ahead)

The game-changing feature:

LIDAR can measure wind before it reaches the turbine!

Why this is amazing:

  • Traditional anemometer: measures wind AT the turbine
  • LIDAR: measures wind 200 meters AHEAD of the turbine
  • Controller can see what's coming!
  • Can adjust blade pitch proactively instead of reactively
  • Smoother power output
  • Less mechanical stress

Advantages:

  • ✓ Measures wind ahead of turbine (predictive control possible)
  • ✓ Can measure at multiple heights simultaneously
  • ✓ Very high precision
  • ✓ 3D wind profile (speed at different heights)
  • ✓ No moving parts

Disadvantages:

  • ✗ Very expensive ($50,000-150,000)
  • ✗ Complex technology
  • ✗ Requires clear air (fog/heavy rain affects it)
  • ✗ High power consumption

Current use:

  • Research turbines
  • High-end installations
  • Wind farm met masts (measuring site wind resources)
  • Increasingly common in offshore wind (where access is expensive)

Future potential:

As costs decrease, LIDAR could become standard. The ability to "see" wind coming enables:

  • Feed-forward control (not just feedback)
  • Individual blade pitch adjustment anticipating turbulence
  • Reduced fatigue loads (gentler on components)
  • Potentially 5-10% more energy capture

5. SODAR (Sonic Detection and Ranging)

Similar concept to LIDAR, but uses sound instead of light:

How it works:

  • Emits focused ultrasonic pulses upward and forward
  • Pulses reflect off air density variations and moving particles
  • Analyzes returned signal using Doppler effect
  • Calculates wind speed at various heights

Advantages:

  • ✓ Less expensive than LIDAR ($20,000-50,000)
  • ✓ Works in fog/rain (unlike LIDAR)
  • ✓ Can measure up to 200m height
  • ✓ Good for wind resource assessment

Disadvantages:

  • ✗ Less precise than LIDAR
  • ✗ Affected by ambient noise
  • ✗ Larger equipment

Typical use:

  • Wind farm site assessment (before turbines installed)
  • Meteorological research
  • Less common on turbines themselves

Wind Direction Measurement - The Wind Vane

While the anemometer tells us wind speed, we also need to know wind direction.

Types of Wind Vanes

There are several designs, each with different output methods:

Analog Output Vane

Design:

  • Traditional weather vane shape (arrow)
  • Mounted on rotating shaft
  • Potentiometer attached to shaft measures rotation angle

Output:

  • Analog voltage (typically 0-10V)
  • Voltage proportional to direction angle
  • 0V = North (or reference direction)
  • 10V = full rotation (360°)
  • Controller reads voltage, calculates angle

Advantages:

  • ✓ Simple
  • ✓ Continuous output
  • ✓ Easy to interpret

Disadvantages:

  • ✗ Potentiometer wears over time
  • ✗ Mechanical connection can fail

Digital Encoder Vane

Design:

  • Wind vane connected to optical or magnetic encoder
  • Encoder directly outputs angle in digital format

Output:

  • Digital data stream (RS-485, CAN bus, etc.)
  • Precise angle reading (0.1° resolution typical)
  • Includes quality/health data

Advantages:

  • ✓ Very accurate
  • ✓ Digital = no conversion errors
  • ✓ Can include diagnostics

Disadvantages:

  • ✗ More expensive
  • ✗ Requires compatible electronics

Duty Cycle Vane (Clever Simple Design)

This is particularly interesting - let's look at it in detail:

[Video demonstration showing duty cycle vane operation]

The mechanical design:

Components:

  • Wind vane (the arrow part that points into wind)
  • Vane oscillates in the wind (like a weather vane that vibrates)
  • Attached to the vane: a semi-circular disc (approximately 180° - 5° = 175°)
  • Fixed to the base (nacelle): an optical sensor (LED + photodetector)

How it works:

The vane naturally oscillates back and forth in the wind (like a pendulum, but excited by wind instead of gravity):

When perfectly aligned with wind:

  • Vane oscillates equally on both sides of center
  • Disc blocks LED light 50% of the time
  • Photodetector output: 50% duty cycle

When nacelle is misaligned:

  • Vane points toward actual wind direction
  • Vane oscillates around this new center position
  • If nacelle is 20° off to the right:
    • Disc blocks light MORE than 50% of the time
    • Maybe 70% duty cycle
  • If nacelle is 20° off to the left:
    • Disc blocks light LESS than 50% of the time
    • Maybe 30% duty cycle

Interpreting the signal:

The Controller sees a square wave (on/off pulses):

[Oscilloscope trace showing duty cycle signal]

Four signal patterns:

The following diagram shows four situations:

[Diagram showing semicircle positions and resulting signals]

Situation 1 (far left):

  • Semicircle never blocks the light sensor
  • Output: Always ON (100% duty cycle)
  • Meaning: Nacelle is severely misaligned (>90° off)

Situation 2:

  • Semicircle blocks sensor only briefly during oscillation
  • Output: Mostly ON (e.g., 80% duty cycle)
  • Meaning: Nacelle is moderately off (perhaps 30-40°)

Situation 3:

  • Semicircle blocks sensor most of the time during oscillation
  • Output: Mostly OFF (e.g., 20% duty cycle)
  • Meaning: Nacelle is moderately off in opposite direction

Situation 4 (far right):

  • Semicircle always blocks the light sensor
  • Output: Always OFF (0% duty cycle)
  • Meaning: Nacelle is severely misaligned in opposite direction

The critical 50% point:

  • When duty cycle = exactly 50%
  • The nacelle is perfectly aligned with wind direction!

Measuring misalignment:

The "misalignment" of the Nacelle relative to wind is given by the difference between:

  • The average angle around which the vane oscillates (actual wind direction)
  • The nacelle's orientation (horizontal reference in the diagram)

Controller signal processing:

The Controller's electronics must:

  1. Detect which of the three patterns:
    • Always ON → severe misalignment one way
    • Always OFF → severe misalignment other way
    • Oscillating → within useful measurement range
  2. If oscillating, measure duty cycle:
    • Time the ON periods
    • Time the total cycle period
    • Calculate: Duty Cycle = (ON time / Total period) × 100%
  3. Convert duty cycle to angle:
    • 50% = perfectly aligned (0° error)
    • Above 50% = misaligned one direction
    • Below 50% = misaligned opposite direction
    • Calibration curve converts duty cycle to degrees

Advantages of duty cycle vane:

  • ✓ Very simple mechanical design
  • ✓ Robust (few parts to fail)
  • ✓ Self-averaging (oscillation smooths out turbulence)
  • ✓ Inexpensive
  • ✓ Easy to replace

Disadvantages:

  • ✗ Only measures relative misalignment (not absolute direction)
  • ✗ Requires signal processing electronics
  • ✗ Less precise than encoder types
  • ✗ Can't distinguish 180° ambiguity (needs initialization)

This design was very common in older turbines and is still used in some models today due to its simplicity and reliability.

Temperature Sensors

Wind turbines monitor temperatures throughout the system. Different sensors are used depending on the requirement:

Simple Bimetallic Sensors (On/Off Type)

Where used:

  • Thyristor/power electronics cooling
  • Yaw motor temperatures
  • Hydraulic pump motors
  • Any component where "too hot" is the only concern

How they work:

The bimetallic principle:

  • Two different metals bonded together
  • Metals expand at different rates when heated
  • Bonded strip bends when temperature changes
  • At calibration temperature, strip bends enough to open/close a switch

Circuit behavior:

  • Below threshold temperature: circuit CLOSED (normal)
  • Above threshold temperature: circuit OPEN (alarm!)
  • Controller sees simple on/off signal

Example circuit:

  • Multiple sensors in series
  • All must be CLOSED for "OK" signal
  • Any ONE opens → Controller sees alarm

Advantages:

  • ✓ Extremely simple
  • ✓ Very reliable
  • ✓ No external power needed
  • ✓ Inexpensive ($5-20 each)
  • ✓ Self-resetting (cools down, contact closes again)

Disadvantages:

  • ✗ No actual temperature reading (just "too hot" or "OK")
  • ✗ Can't trend data
  • ✗ Limited to one threshold
  • ✗ Doesn't tell you HOW hot

Typical thresholds:

  • Power electronics: 70-80°C
  • Motor windings: 120-140°C
  • Hydraulic oil: 80-90°C

Precision PT100/PT1000 Sensors

For critical components needing accurate monitoring:

Where used:

  • Generator windings - most critical!
  • Gearbox oil - temperature affects lubrication
  • Rotor bearing - early warning of problems
  • Ambient air - affects cooling capacity
  • Any component where trending and precise monitoring needed

What are PT100/PT1000 sensors?

The "PT" part:

  • PT = Platinum
  • Platinum's electrical resistance changes predictably with temperature
  • Very stable, repeatable relationship
  • Industry standard for precision temperature measurement

The number:

  • PT100: 100 ohms at 0°C
  • PT1000: 1000 ohms at 0°C
  • PT1000 gives better resolution (10× more resistance change)

The physics:

Platinum resistance increases with temperature:

  • Temperature Coefficient: 0.385 ohms/°C (for PT100)
  • At 0°C: 100.00 ohms
  • At 100°C: 138.50 ohms (100 + 100 × 0.385)
  • Relationship is nearly linear (slight curve at extremes)

The measurement challenge:

Problem 1: Cable resistance

The sensor is often far from the Controller:

  • PT100 sensor: 100 ohms
  • Cable to sensor: maybe 5 ohms (each wire!)
  • Total measured: 110 ohms
  • Huge error! (looks like +26°C error!)

Problem 2: Measurement current heating

To measure resistance, you must pass current through it:

  • Current creates I²R heating
  • Heats the sensor itself
  • Falsifies the reading!
  • Must use very low current (typically <1 mA)

Solutions - 3-Wire and 4-Wire Measurement

These are standard industrial techniques. Let's understand them:

3-Wire Method (Most Common in Wind Turbines)

[Fig 1: 3-wire PT100 circuit diagram]

The setup:

  • Three wires connect sensor to measurement device
  • R₁, R₂, R₃ = resistance of the three wires
  • R_PT = the PT100 sensor we want to measure
  • L1, L2, L3 = connection terminals at the Controller

How it works:

By design, the three wires are identical (same length, same gauge):

  • R₁ ≈ R₂ ≈ R₃

The clever trick:

  1. Measure resistance between L3 and L2:
    • Reading₁ = R₃ + R_PT + R₂
  2. Measure resistance between L2 and L1:
    • Reading₂ = R₂ + R₁
  3. Calculate the actual sensor resistance:
    • R_PT = Reading₁ - Reading₂
    • R_PT = (R₃ + R_PT + R₂) - (R₂ + R₁)
    • Since R₃ ≈ R₁, they cancel out!
    • Result: R_PT (cable resistance eliminated!)

Accuracy:

  • Excellent if the three wires are truly identical
  • Small differences in R₁, R₂, R₃ create small errors
  • Typical accuracy: ±0.1°C to ±0.5°C
  • Good enough for most wind turbine applications

4-Wire Method (Highest Precision)

[Fig: 4-wire PT100 circuit]

The setup:

  • Four wires connect sensor to measurement device
  • Two wires carry measurement current (I)
  • Two wires measure voltage (V)

How it works:

The brilliance:

The voltage measurement device has VERY high impedance (>1 MΩ):

  • Essentially zero current flows through voltage sense wires (L2 and L3)
  • Therefore, essentially zero voltage drop across R₂ and R₃
  • Measured voltage = voltage directly across R_PT
  • No cable resistance error at all!

The process:

  1. Pass known current through L1 → R_PT → L4
    • Some voltage dropped across R₁ (don't care)
    • Some voltage dropped across R_PT (this is what we want!)
    • Some voltage dropped across R₄ (don't care)
  2. High-impedance voltmeter measures L2 to L3
    • Reads voltage across R_PT only
    • No current through R₂ and R₃ = no error!
  3. Calculate resistance:
    • R_PT = V / I (Ohm's law)
    • Convert resistance to temperature using calibration table

Accuracy:

  • Excellent: ±0.01°C to ±0.1°C
  • Limited by sensor quality, not cable
  • Used in laboratory and critical applications

Why not always use 4-wire?

  • ✗ Requires 4 conductors (more expensive cable)
  • ✗ More complex wiring
  • ✗ For wind turbines, 3-wire is usually accurate enough

Minimizing Self-Heating Error

Both 3-wire and 4-wire methods help with cable resistance, but we still have the self-heating issue.

Techniques used:

1. Low measurement current:

  • Typical: 0.5 to 1.0 mA
  • Power dissipated: I² × R = (0.001)² × 100 = 0.0001 W
  • Tiny heating effect
  • Trade-off: lower current = smaller voltage signal = more noise susceptible

2. Pulsed measurement:

  • Some systems don't measure continuously
  • Send brief current pulse (10-100 ms)
  • Measure voltage during pulse
  • Turn off current between measurements
  • Sensor cools between pulses
  • Virtually eliminates self-heating error

3. Current reversal:

  • Measure with current in one direction
  • Measure with current in opposite direction
  • Average the two readings
  • Cancels some error sources (thermoelectric effects)

PT100 Installation Best Practices

For reliable measurements:

Sensor placement:

  • Direct contact with surface being measured
  • Thermal paste/compound improves contact
  • Secure mounting (vibration can cause intermittent contact)
  • Shield from air currents if measuring surface temperature

Cable routing:

  • Keep cables away from high-current power cables
  • Electrical noise can affect readings
  • Use shielded cable in electrically noisy environments
  • Proper grounding of shield

Calibration:

  • New sensors: typically ±0.3°C accuracy (Class B)
  • High-grade sensors: ±0.1°C (Class A)
  • Periodic calibration verification recommended
  • Check against known reference (ice bath = 0°C, boiling water = 100°C)

Temperature Monitoring in Practice

Generator Temperature Monitoring:

Typically uses 3-6 PT100 sensors:

  • One per phase winding (3 sensors)
  • Bearing temperatures (2-3 sensors)
  • Ambient air in generator (1 sensor)

What the Controller does:

Normal operation:

  • Samples every 1-10 seconds
  • Compares to thresholds
  • Trends over time (looking for unusual rise rates)

Warning level (e.g., 120°C):

  • Increase cooling fan speed
  • Reduce power output slightly
  • Alert maintenance system

Alarm level (e.g., 140°C):

  • Immediate power reduction
  • If doesn't decrease: initiate cutout
  • Record event for investigation

Critical level (e.g., 155°C):

  • Emergency shutdown
  • Major alarm
  • Require manual reset after investigation

Why this matters:

Generator rewind (if windings damaged by overheating):

  • Cost: $50,000-200,000
  • Downtime: 4-8 weeks
  • Much better to catch overheating early!
