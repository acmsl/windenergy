Practical Lab 5: Emergency Stops and Fault Responses

Objective:

Understand how the turbine responds to fault conditions and emergency situations. You'll learn:

  • Difference between normal stop and emergency stop
  • Various fault conditions that trigger automatic shutdown
  • The emergency shutdown sequence (faster, harder on equipment)
  • How different faults are detected and handled
  • Controller's decision-making during faults
  • Recovery procedures after emergency stops
  • Safety system hierarchy and redundancy

Background - Why Emergency Stops Exist

Normal Operation:

In Labs 1-4, we saw controlled, gentle procedures:

  • Gradual power reduction (30+ seconds)
  • Gentle electrical disconnection
  • Aerodynamic braking first
  • Mechanical brake at low speed
  • Prioritizes equipment protection

Emergency Situations:

But sometimes, conditions require immediate action:

  • Equipment at risk of damage
  • Safety hazard present
  • Grid fault
  • Component failure
  • Overspeed condition

In these cases: Speed matters more than gentleness!

The Trade-off:

Normal stop:

  • Takes ~60 seconds
  • Very gentle on equipment
  • Minimal stress
  • Use when time permits

Emergency stop:

  • Takes ~10-20 seconds
  • Harder on equipment
  • Higher stress
  • Use when necessary for safety

Think of it like a car:

  • Normal stop: Gradual braking over 100 meters
  • Emergency stop: Slam on brakes (hard, but prevents accident!)

Types of Faults and Triggers

Let's categorize the different emergency conditions:

Category 1: Overspeed Faults (Most Critical)

VCU (Velocity Centrifugal Unit) - Generator Shaft:

  • Mechanical overspeed detector
  • Mounted on high-speed shaft (generator shaft)
  • Trip point: ~1,803 RPM (for 1500 RPM sync Large Generator)
    • That's 20% above sync speed
    • Indicates something seriously wrong!
  • Action: Immediate emergency shutdown

HCU (High-speed Centrifugal Unit) - Rotor Shaft:

  • Backup overspeed detector
  • Mounted on low-speed shaft (rotor shaft)
  • Trip point: ~26 RPM (for Large Generator operation)
  • Action: Immediate emergency shutdown
  • Redundancy: Catches overspeed if VCU fails

Why overspeed is critical:

  • Centrifugal forces increase with speed²
  • At 130% rated speed → forces are 1.69× normal
  • Blade can break apart (catastrophic!)
  • Gearbox can destroy itself
  • Generator can fail explosively
  • Must stop IMMEDIATELY

Category 2: Grid Faults

Phase Loss:

  • One or more grid phases drop to zero
  • Causes single-phasing (very dangerous for generator)
  • Unbalanced currents → overheating
  • Action: Immediate disconnect

Over/Under Voltage:

  • Grid voltage outside acceptable range
  • Under-voltage: <620V per phase (typical)
  • Over-voltage: >760V per phase (typical)
  • Can damage generator insulation
  • Action: Disconnect after brief delay (2-5 sec)

Frequency Deviation:

  • Grid frequency outside 49.5-50.5 Hz (for 50 Hz grid)
  • Or 59.5-60.5 Hz (for 60 Hz grid)
  • Indicates major grid instability
  • Action: Disconnect immediately

Category 3: Temperature Faults

Generator Overheating:

  • Winding temperature exceeds safe limit
  • Warning: >140°C
  • Critical: >155°C
  • Can damage insulation, cause failure
  • Action: Shutdown (emergency if >155°C)

Gearbox Overheating:

  • Oil temperature excessive
  • Warning: >85°C
  • Critical: >95°C
  • Can indicate bearing failure or oil loss
  • Action: Shutdown (emergency if >95°C)

Thyristor Overheating:

  • Power electronics overheating
  • Can cause semiconductor failure
  • Multiple bimetallic sensors
  • Action: Immediate shutdown if any trips

Category 4: Mechanical Faults

Excessive Vibration:

  • Tower vibration sensor triggers
  • Could indicate:
    • Blade damage/imbalance
    • Bearing failure
    • Foundation problem
    • Ice accumulation on blades
  • Action: Immediate shutdown, investigation needed

Brake System Fault:

  • Can't verify brake release
  • Can't verify brake application
  • Hydraulic pressure fault
  • Safety-critical system
  • Action: Shutdown, cannot operate safely

Pitch System Fault:

  • Blade pitch not responding to commands
  • Difference between commanded and actual >5° for >10 sec
  • Cannot control power extraction
  • Action: Emergency pitch to feather (90°), then stop

Category 5: Control System Faults

Sensor Failures:

  • Anemometer failure (can't measure wind)
  • Speed sensor failure (can't measure RPM)
  • Wind vane failure (can't orient correctly)
  • Action: Shutdown, cannot operate blind

Communication Loss:

  • Connection to remote SCADA lost
  • Depends on configuration (may continue or stop)

Controller Self-Diagnostic Failure:

  • Internal software error detected
  • Watchdog timer expired
  • Memory corruption
  • Action: Safe shutdown, needs reset

Preparation

1. Starting State - Normal Production

For most fault scenarios, we need the turbine running:

Setup:

  • Set wind speed: 10 m/s (good production wind)
  • Start turbine (if not already running)
  • Wait for normal production
  • Verify:
    • State: "Production Large"
    • Power: ~900-1,000 kW
    • Generator speed: ~1,510 RPM
    • All systems normal

2. Observation Setup

This lab involves fast sequences - be ready!

Arrange windows to see:

  • State Machine display (watch state transitions)
  • Power output graph (see rapid drop)
  • RPM indicators (blade and generator)
  • Incident log (faults appear quickly)
  • Emergency stop indicators
  • Brake status

3. Recording Preparation

Emergency stops happen fast:

  • Have video recording ready (if possible)
  • Or take detailed notes quickly
  • Focus on: timing, sequence, final state

4. Safety Note

Even in simulation, think like a real operator:

  • Emergency stops protect equipment
  • In real turbine, emergency stop is serious event
  • Always investigate cause before restarting
  • Never dismiss emergency stops as "normal"

Procedure Part A: Overspeed Emergency Stop

This is the most critical fault - let's trigger it safely in the simulator.

Phase 1: Create Overspeed Condition

Warning: In a real turbine, this would never be done intentionally! We can do it safely only in simulation.

Method 1: Disable Pitch Control (If Simulator Allows)

Setup:

  1. Turbine in normal production (10 m/s, ~1,000 kW)
  2. If simulator has fault injection:
    • Select "Pitch System Failure"
    • Pitch freezes at current angle (~2°)
  3. Now increase wind speed dramatically:
    • From 10 m/s to 20 m/s
    • Do it gradually (10 seconds)

What happens:

Without pitch control:

  • Blade angle can't increase (stuck at 2°)
  • Wind speed increasing
  • Power extraction increases uncontrollably
  • Torque on generator shaft increases
  • Generator speed increases!

Phase 2: VCU Activation

At 1,803 RPM:

What happens (all nearly simultaneous):

VCU mechanical switch activates:

  • Centrifugal weights fly outward
  • Mechanical switch closes
  • Signal sent to Controller: "OVERSPEED!"

Incident created:

  • Description: "Overspeed Detected - VCU Activated"
  • Start time: Exact moment
  • Severity: CRITICAL
  • This is a major fault!

State Machine:

  • Security State: IMMEDIATE transition to emergency state
  • Operation State: "Production Large" → "Emergency Shutdown"
  • Last Event: "EOverspeedVCU"

Record exact time: When did VCU trip?

Phase 3: Emergency Shutdown Sequence

Now watch the emergency response - it's much faster than normal stop!

Step 3.1 - Immediate Actions (First 0.5 seconds)

Multiple simultaneous commands:

1. Pitch to feather - FAST:

  • Command: Blade pitch to 90° (feathered)
  • Rate: MAXIMUM (not gentle 1°/sec like normal)
  • Might be 10-15°/sec!
  • Goal: Stop aerodynamic power input NOW

2. Electrical disconnect - IMMEDIATE:

  • Open bypass contactors (no waiting for zero power!)
  • Open generator contactor
  • Complete electrical isolation
  • Even if power still flowing (creates arc, but necessary)

3. Brake application - FAST:

  • Close hydraulic valve (dump pressure)
  • Mechanical brake applies
  • Even though speed is high (not ideal, but necessary)
  • Spring force slams pads onto disc

Step 3.2 - Deceleration Phase

Combined braking:

Aerodynamic braking:

  • Pitch increasing rapidly toward 90°
  • At high pitch (>60°), Cp becomes very negative
  • Blades acting as strong air brake
  • Extracts energy from rotation

Mechanical braking:

  • Brake pads gripping disc
  • Friction converts kinetic energy to heat
  • Brake disc temperature rising rapidly!
  • Might see smoke (in real turbine)

Why So Harsh?

At overspeed:

  • Risk of blade failure (catastrophic)
  • Risk of gearbox destruction
  • Risk of generator explosion
  • Risk of tower collapse (worst case)

Equipment damage is acceptable if it prevents catastrophic failure!

Better to:

  • Replace worn brake pads (€2,000)
  • Than replace broken gearbox (€300,000)
  • Or worse - collapsed turbine (total loss)

Brake Stress:

Normal stop from 5 RPM:

  • Kinetic energy in rotor: E = 0.5 × I × ω²
  • ω = 5 RPM × 2π/60 = 0.524 rad/sec
  • I (moment of inertia) = 3,500,000 kg·m²
  • E = 0.5 × 3,500,000 × (0.524)² ≈ 481,000 Joules

Emergency stop from 1,803 RPM (generator):

  • Generator ω = 1,803 RPM × 2π/60 = 188.8 rad/sec
  • Rotor ω = 188.8 / 77.73 = 2.43 rad/sec (through gearbox)
  • But most energy in high-speed shaft and gearbox!
  • Total kinetic energy ≈ 20-30× higher than normal stop

This energy becomes heat in brake pads!

No wonder temperature rises so much!

Procedure Part B: Grid Phase Loss

Another critical fault - let's see how turbine responds.

Phase 1: Create Grid Fault

Setup:

  1. Reset from previous emergency (clear incidents, restart turbine)
  2. Get back to normal production (10 m/s, ~1,000 kW)
  3. All three grid phases present initially

Trigger fault:

If simulator has fault injection:

  • Select "Grid Phase 1 Loss"
  • Phase 1 voltage drops to 0V
  • Immediately!

If manual control:

  • Disable Phase 1 power supply
  • Or set Phase 1 voltage to 0V

Phase 2: Fault Detection

Within milliseconds:

Voltage monitoring:

  • Controller reads phase voltages every few milliseconds
  • Phase 1: 690V → 0V (sudden drop)
  • Phase 2: 690V (still OK)
  • Phase 3: 690V (still OK)

Fault recognized:

  • Missing phase detected!
  • This is serious - single-phasing

Why single-phasing is dangerous:

With only two phases:

  • Generator still tries to produce power
  • But current is unbalanced
  • Some windings carry 2× normal current
  • Rapid overheating
  • Can destroy generator in minutes!

Incident created:

  • Description: "Grid Phase 1 Voltage Lost"
  • Time: Exact moment of loss
  • Severity: CRITICAL

State transition:

  • Operation State: "Production Large" → "Emergency Cutout - Grid Fault"
  • Security State: → "Grid Fault Emergency"

Phase 3: Emergency Disconnect

Unlike overspeed, this is slightly less rushed:

The turbine itself isn't overspeeding, but must disconnect from faulty grid immediately.

Sequence:

Step 1: Electrical disconnect (immediate - 0.5 sec)

  • Open bypass contactors
  • Open generator contactor
  • Electrical isolation achieved
  • Generator now freewheeling

Step 2: Power reduction (fast but controlled - 10 sec)

  • Increase pitch rapidly
  • From ~2° to ~45°
  • Faster than normal (maybe 4-5°/sec)
  • Power already zero (disconnected), but reducing torque

Step 3: Mechanical stop (standard - 20 sec)

  • Continue pitch to 90°
  • Aerodynamic braking
  • Mechanical brake at ~200-300 RPM
  • Complete stop

Total time: ~30-35 seconds (faster than normal, slower than overspeed)

Why intermediate speed?

  • Not as critical as overspeed (no immediate catastrophic risk)
  • But serious enough to warrant fast response
  • Protects generator from single-phasing damage
  • Balance: safety vs. equipment wear

Method 2: Direct Temperature Manipulation

Faster method:

  1. Set "Generator Temperature" directly to 156°C
  2. Immediately triggers alarm

Phase 2: Temperature Alarm Triggers

At 155°C (alarm threshold):

Controller logic:

  • Temperature sensor reads >155°C
  • This is CRITICAL level
  • Can damage winding insulation
  • Action required NOW

Incident created:

  • Description: "Generator Temperature Excessive - Emergency Shutdown"
  • Temperature: 155°C (or current reading)
  • Severity: CRITICAL

State transition:

  • Operation State: → "Emergency Shutdown - Overtemperature"
  • Security State: → "Thermal Emergency"

Phase 3: Emergency Thermal Shutdown

This is more controlled than overspeed:

Step 1: Electrical disconnect (relatively fast - 5 sec)

  • Reduce power quickly (pitch to ~20°)
  • When power <50 kW: disconnect
  • Removes heat source (no more electrical losses)

Step 2: Continue to full stop (standard - 40 sec)

  • Pitch to 90°
  • Aerodynamic and mechanical braking
  • Stop completely

Step 3: Cooling period

  • Turbine stopped
  • Generator cooling fans keep running (if not failed!)
  • Temperature gradually decreases
  • Monitor: 155°C → 140°C → 120°C → 100°C...

Total shutdown time: ~45 seconds (controlled, not rushed)

Recovery:

Temperature must drop below safe threshold before restart:

  • Typical requirement: <100°C
  • With fans running: ~15-30 minutes to cool
  • Without fans (if failed): hours!

Incident requires:

  • Acknowledgment
  • Investigation of cause
  • Resolution (fix cooling fan if failed)
  • Manual restart approval

Procedure Part D: Excessive Vibration

Phase 1: Create Vibration Condition

Realistic causes (simulator might model):

  • Blade damage (chip, crack)
  • Ice accumulation (unbalanced)
  • Bearing failure
  • Foundation issue

Simulation: If simulator has vibration injection:

  1. Select "Tower Vibration - High"
  2. Accelerometer reading increases
  3. Exceeds threshold

Phase 2: Vibration Detection

Tower accelerometer:

  • Measures vibration amplitude
  • Units: mm/sec or g-forces
  • Normal: <10 mm/sec
  • Warning: 10-20 mm/sec
  • Alarm: >20 mm/sec

At alarm threshold:

Incident:

  • Description: "Excessive Tower Vibration Detected"
  • Level: 25 mm/sec (example)
  • Severity: CRITICAL

Concern:

  • Indicates something seriously wrong
  • Could be:
    • Imminent blade failure
    • Bearing disintegrating
    • Foundation cracking
  • Must stop immediately and investigate

Phase 3: Emergency Vibration Shutdown

Sequence (similar to grid fault):

Step 1: Fast electrical disconnect (0.5 sec)

Step 2: Rapid mechanical stop (20-30 sec)

  • Fast pitch to feather
  • Brake at moderate speed
  • Stop as quickly as safely possible

Step 3: Inspection hold

  • Incident requires investigation
  • Cannot restart until cause found
  • Might require nacelle access
  • Visual blade inspection
  • Bearing diagnostics

This is one fault that always requires human intervention before restart!

Procedure Part E: Manual Emergency Stop Button

Physical E-Stop:

Most turbines have physical emergency stop button(s):

  • One at tower base (always)
  • Sometimes in nacelle
  • Sometimes at remote location
  • Big red button, cannot miss it!

When pressed:

Immediate actions:

  • Main circuit breaker opens (if accessible electrically)
  • Emergency shutdown signal to Controller
  • Overrides all other control logic
  • Fastest possible stop initiated

Response (similar to overspeed):

  • Immediate electrical disconnect
  • Fast pitch to feather
  • Brake application (even if high speed)
  • Complete stop ASAP

Cannot restart until:

  • E-stop button released
  • Incident acknowledged
  • Investigation completed
  • Manual authorization given

Purpose:

  • Personnel safety
  • Fire
  • Observed damage
  • Any immediate danger
  • Ultimate safety override

Common Elements:

All emergency stops include:

  1. Immediate electrical disconnect (don't wait for zero power)
  2. Faster pitch change rate than normal
  3. Brake application at higher speed than ideal
  4. Critical incident created
  5. Requires investigation before restart
  6. Manual acknowledgment required

Decision Tree - Stop Speed Selection:

Controller logic:

IF fault = Overspeed OR Manual E-Stop THEN

    Emergency_Level = MAXIMUM

    Pitch_Rate = Maximum (10-15°/sec)

    Brake_At_Speed = Current speed (even if high)

    Target_Time = <20 seconds

    

ELSE IF fault = Grid_Phase_Loss OR Vibration THEN

    Emergency_Level = HIGH

    Pitch_Rate = Fast (4-6°/sec)

    Brake_At_Speed = <300 RPM

    Target_Time = <30 seconds

    

ELSE IF fault = Overtemperature OR Warning_Level THEN

    Emergency_Level = MEDIUM

    Pitch_Rate = Moderate (2-3°/sec)

    Brake_At_Speed = <100 RPM

    Target_Time = <45 seconds

    

ELSE (Normal stop)

    Emergency_Level = NONE

    Pitch_Rate = Gentle (1°/sec)

    Brake_At_Speed = <10 RPM

    Target_Time = ~60 seconds

END IF

The hierarchy balances:

  • Safety (prevent catastrophic failure)
  • Equipment protection (minimize wear)
  • Production continuity (don't stop unless necessary)

Recovery Procedures

After emergency stop, cannot simply press START!

Standard Recovery Process:

Step 1: Investigate (Required)

Questions to answer:

  • What caused the fault?
  • Is it still present?
  • Was any damage done?
  • Is it safe to restart?

For each fault type:

Overspeed:

  • Why did speed go too high?
  • Pitch control failure?
  • Wind gust beyond design?
  • Control system error?
  • Requires thorough investigation

Grid phase loss:

  • Did utility have fault?
  • Is grid restored now?
  • All three phases present?
  • Voltages stable?
  • Check grid, usually can restart when grid OK

Overtemperature:

  • Has temperature dropped <100°C?
  • Why did it overheat?
  • Cooling fan working now?
  • Ambient temperature too high?
  • Fix cooling system if failed, wait for cool-down

Vibration:

  • Visual inspection of blades
  • Check for ice, damage
  • Bearing diagnostics
  • Foundation inspection
  • Must find and fix cause

Step 2: Clear Faults

In Control Terminal:

  • Review all incidents in log
  • Understand each one
  • Check "OK" box to acknowledge
  • This confirms: "I investigated and understand"

Step 3: Reset Systems (If Needed)

Some faults require manual reset:

  • VCU/HCU: Might need physical reset
  • E-stop button: Must be released
  • Protection relays: Might need reset
  • Controller: Might need restart

Step 4: Verify Conditions

Before attempting restart:

  • ✓ All faults cleared
  • ✓ All incidents acknowledged
  • ✓ Cause identified and resolved
  • ✓ Wind in acceptable range
  • ✓ Grid available (if grid fault)
  • ✓ Temperature normal (if temp fault)
  • ✓ No vibration (if vibration fault)

Step 5: Test Restart (Cautious)

First attempt:

  • Press START
  • Monitor closely during startup
  • Watch for fault recurrence
  • Ready to stop if problem returns

If fault recurs:

  • STOP immediately
  • Investigation incomplete
  • Do NOT keep trying
  • Needs expert diagnosis

If startup normal:

  • Continue to production
  • Monitor closely for several hours
  • Log the incident in maintenance records
  • Schedule detailed inspection if needed

Observations and Questions

Things to Notice:

1. Speed of response:

Emergency stops happen FAST:

  • Fault detected: <0.1 second
  • Electrical disconnect: <0.5 seconds
  • Full stop: <30 seconds
  • Compare to: Normal startup takes 2-4 minutes!

2. Multiple sensors for critical parameters:

Overspeed detection has redundancy:

  • VCU on generator shaft (primary)
  • HCU on rotor shaft (backup)
  • Speed calculation from position sensors
  • Multiple independent systems

3. State machine complexity:

Security State Machine has many emergency states:

  • Normal operation
  • Warning level
  • Alarm level
  • Emergency shutdown
  • Critical emergency
  • Each with different behaviors

4. Cannot restart from emergency state:

After emergency stop:

  • State: "STOP (Emergency)"
  • START button might be grayed out or ineffective
  • Must clear faults first
  • Must return to normal "STOP" state
  • Then can attempt restart

Questions to Explore:

Q1: What happens if multiple faults occur simultaneously?

Try it:

  • Trigger overspeed AND grid phase loss at same time
  • Which takes priority?
  • How does Controller handle multiple emergencies?
  • Check incident log - what order are they recorded?

Q2: Can you stop an emergency stop in progress?

Try it:

  • Trigger emergency stop (any type)
  • During shutdown sequence, press STOP button again
  • Does anything change?
  • Can you press START to cancel? (Don't think so!)
  • Emergency stops are non-cancellable?

Q3: How often can emergency stops be performed?

Think about it:

  • Each emergency stop stresses equipment
  • Brake pads wear
  • Brake disc heats up
  • If done repeatedly, could:
    • Overheat brake (>300°C → damage)
    • Wear pads completely
    • Damage brake disc
  • Real limit: Maybe 3-5 emergency stops before brake inspection needed

Q4: What if brake fails during emergency stop?

Extreme scenario:

  • Overspeed condition
  • Brake hydraulic system failed (no pressure)
  • Brake cannot apply!
  • What happens?
  • Only aerodynamic braking available (pitch to 90°)
  • Slower stop (maybe 60-90 seconds)
  • Speed might reach even higher before stopping
  • Very dangerous situation!

Experiment 2: Stress Test - Repeated Emergency Stops

  4. Clear faults

  • Typical: 1-2 minutes (just clear incident and verify grid)

Overtemperature:

  • Trigger fault (156°C)
  • Turbine stops
  • How long to cool to <100°C?
  • Monitor temperature vs. time
  • Calculate cooling rate: °C per minute
  • Typical: 15-30 minutes

Vibration:

  • Trigger fault
  • Turbine stops
  • In real turbine: would need physical inspection (hours)
  • In simulator: might just need to clear fault

Overspeed:

  • Trigger fault
  • Turbine stops
  • In real turbine: extensive investigation (hours to days)
  • Check VCU reset
  • Inspect gearbox for damage
  • Test all systems before restart

Experiment 4: Fault Progression - Warning to Emergency

Some faults have warning stages before emergency:

Temperature example:

  1. Start at 85°C (normal)
  2. Simulate cooling failure
  3. Watch temperature rise
  4. Note when warnings occur:
    • 120°C: Informational log entry?
    • 140°C: Warning incident created?
    • 155°C: Emergency shutdown

Does Controller try to avoid emergency?

  • At 140°C: Does it reduce power to slow heating?
  • Does it give time for operator intervention?
  • Or does it wait until 155°C critical threshold?

Test it:

  • See if you can manually reduce power after warning
  • Prevent reaching emergency threshold
  • Save the emergency stop!

Common Issues and Troubleshooting

Issue 1: "Nuisance Trips"

Symptom:

  • Emergency stops occurring frequently
  • But no real fault found
  • Happens repeatedly
  • Lost production is significant

Possible causes:

  • Sensor malfunction (false readings)
  • Threshold set too conservatively
  • Environmental conditions (lightning causing electrical noise)
  • Worn components triggering genuine but marginal faults

Example: Vibration sensor too sensitive

  • Triggers at 20 mm/sec threshold
  • Normal operation occasionally reaches 19 mm/sec
  • Any slight increase → emergency stop
  • But no actual damage occurring

Diagnosis:

  • Review fault log history
  • Are faults always same type?
  • Are threshold values appropriate?
  • Check sensor calibration

Solution:

  • Recalibrate sensor if faulty
  • Adjust threshold if too conservative (carefully!)
  • Add filtering to sensor signal
  • Replace sensor if failing

Issue 2: "Cannot Restart After Emergency"

Symptom:

  • Emergency stop occurred
  • Fault cause resolved
  • Incidents acknowledged
  • But START button still doesn't work

Possible causes:

  • Residual fault condition still present
  • Emergency state not fully cleared
  • Hardware interlock not reset
  • Controller logic stuck

Diagnosis:

  • Check ALL incidents (might have missed one)
  • Verify actual physical state matches what Controller expects
  • Check all sensor readings
  • Look for hardware fault indicators

Example: VCU not reset

  • VCU tripped
  • Mechanical switch still open
  • Needs physical reset (push button or rotate shaft)
  • Controller sees "VCU active" even though speed now 0
  • Won't allow restart

Solution:

  • Physical reset of VCU/HCU if needed
  • Verify all sensors reading correctly
  • Might need Controller restart (power cycle)
  • Check for hardware interlocks

Issue 3: "Brake Damage After Emergency Stop"

Symptom:

  • Emergency stop from high speed
  • Now brake squealing or grinding
  • Vibration when braking
  • Brake temperature sensor reading high even when stopped

Possible causes:

  • Brake pads glazed (overheated)
  • Brake disc warped (thermal stress)
  • Brake pad material worn significantly
  • Brake disc hot spots

Diagnosis:

  • Check brake temperature history
  • Did it exceed 300°C during stop?
  • Visual inspection of brake (if possible)
  • Test brake holding force

Solution:

  • In real turbine: Immediate inspection required
  • Might need pad replacement
  • Might need disc resurfacing or replacement
  • Cost: €5,000-20,000 depending on damage
  • Downtime: Days (waiting for parts, crane access)

This is why emergency stops are avoided when possible!

Issue 4: "Generator Damage After Grid Fault"

Symptom:

  • Grid phase loss occurred
  • Emergency stop executed
  • Grid restored
  • Turbine restarted
  • But generator making unusual noise
  • Lower power output than expected
  • Generator temperature higher than before

Possible causes:

  • Single-phasing damaged winding before disconnect
  • Unbalanced currents caused overheating
  • Partial winding failure
  • Insulation damage

Diagnosis:

  • Compare power output before/after
  • Check generator winding resistance (all three phases equal?)
  • Insulation resistance test
  • Thermal imaging of generator

Solution:

  • Might need generator rewind
  • Cost: €50,000-200,000
  • Downtime: Weeks
  • Preventable: Faster fault detection and disconnect

This is why grid monitoring is so critical!

Advanced Topic: Fault Ride-Through Requirements

Modern Grid Codes:

Recent regulations require turbines to:

  • NOT disconnect during some grid faults
  • Support grid stability
  • Called "Fault Ride-Through" (FRT)
  • Or "Low Voltage Ride Through" (LVRT)

The Challenge:

Our turbine (older technology):

  • Phase loss → immediate disconnect
  • Under-voltage → disconnect
  • Over-voltage → disconnect
  • Simple, protective strategy

Modern turbines (with power converters):

  • Can stay connected during grid faults
  • Provide reactive power support
  • Help grid recover
  • More complex control

Example LVRT Requirement:

Grid voltage drops to 50% for 2 seconds:

  • Old turbine: Would disconnect
  • Modern turbine: Must stay connected
    • Provide reactive current
    • Support voltage recovery
    • Disconnect only if fault persists >3 seconds

Why the Change?

With high wind penetration:

  • If all turbines disconnect during grid fault
  • Massive power loss
  • Grid becomes more unstable
  • Cascading failures possible

Better:

  • Turbines stay connected
  • Support grid through fault
  • Grid recovers faster
  • More stable overall

Implications:

For our turbine type:

  • Cannot meet modern grid codes
  • OK for older installations (grandfathered)
  • Not suitable for new installations
  • Being replaced by modern variable-speed turbines

This is one reason why:

  • Fixed-speed, direct-coupled turbines declining
  • Variable-speed with converters becoming standard
  • More complex, but better grid integration

Safety System Philosophy

Defense in Depth:

Multiple layers of protection:

Layer 1: Control System

  • Monitors all parameters continuously
  • Responds to abnormal conditions
  • Adjusts operation to avoid faults
  • Example: Reduce power if temperature rising

Layer 2: Automatic Shutdowns

  • Executes emergency stop if Layer 1 fails
  • Threshold-based (exceed limit → stop)
  • Fast response
  • Example: Overspeed → VCU triggers

Layer 3: Mechanical Safety Systems

  • Independent of electrical control
  • Mechanical overspeed switches (VCU/HCU)
  • Spring-applied brakes (fail-safe)
  • Work even if controller fails

Layer 4: Physical Limits

  • Ultimate backstop
  • Blade designed to fail safely (fold back, not shatter)
  • Tower designed for worst-case loads
  • Foundation overdesigned

Redundancy Examples:

Overspeed protection:

  • Primary: Controller monitors speed sensor
  • Backup 1: VCU on generator shaft (mechanical)
  • Backup 2: HCU on rotor shaft (mechanical)
  • Backup 3: Aerodynamic overspeed limitation (blade stalls)

Brake system:

  • Primary: Controlled brake application (hydraulic)
  • Backup 1: Spring force (if hydraulic fails)
  • Backup 2: Aerodynamic braking (pitch to 90°)
  • Backup 3: Blade stall at extreme speeds

Why So Much Redundancy?

Wind turbines operate:

  • Unmanned most of the time
  • In harsh environments (extreme weather)
  • High power levels (dangerous if uncontrolled)
  • Remote locations (help not immediately available)

Must be able to:

  • Detect faults autonomously
  • Respond safely without human intervention
  • Fail to safe state
  • Protect people and equipment
