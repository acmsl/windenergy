Introduction: The power curve

📖 Fundamental concept: Power vs Wind Speed curve

The power curve is a graphical representation showing the relationship between wind speed and the electrical power generated by a wind turbine.

This curve is the "fingerprint" of each wind turbine and is fundamental for:

  • Estimating annual energy production
  • Comparing the performance of different technologies
  • Optimising design according to site conditions
  • Evaluating the return on investment of the project

Characteristics of ideal curves

The ideal Power vs Wind Speed curves for the different wind turbine families are described in detail in the tutorial here.

⚡ Key difference between technologies

One of the main differences between wind turbine families is the effectiveness of energy transfer when wind speed is below the Rated Wind Speed.

In this operating zone, the ability to efficiently extract energy from the wind largely determines the wind turbine's annual energy production.

Characteristic zones of the power curve

📊 Regions of the power curve

A typical power curve has four characteristic zones:

  1. Zone I – Below cut-in: Insufficient speed, wind turbine stopped (Power = 0)
  2. Zone II – Between cut-in and rated speed: Rising power, zone of maximum energy capture
  3. Zone III – Between rated speed and cut-out: Constant power (Rated Power)
  4. Zone IV – Above cut-out: Wind turbine stopped for safety reasons (Power = 0)

Experiment objective

🔬 Experimental goal

The aim of this experiment is to create comparative charts of Power vs Wind Speed curves for the three main wind turbine families:

  • DSAS (Direct Stall Active Stall)
  • RRC (Rotor Resistance Control)
  • DFIG (Doubly Fed Induction Generator)

The analysis will focus especially on Zone II, where the greatest performance differences are observed.

Experiment methodology

Step 3: Data collection

For each wind turbine:

  1. Set the wind speed (start from cut-in up to speeds above rated)
  2. Wait until the wind turbine reaches stable operation
  3. Record the power generated in steady state
  4. Increase wind speed in regular steps (recommended: 0.5 or 1 m/s)

Data normalisation: Per-unit values (p.u.)

⚠️ Critical note on comparison

The three wind turbines have different Rated Power values, which makes direct comparison difficult. Therefore, it is necessary to normalise the data using per-unit (p.u.) values.

📐 Calculating per-unit values

To obtain per-unit (p.u.) values, divide each observed power value by the Rated Power of the corresponding wind turbine:

Power (p.u.) = Measured Power (kW) / Rated Power (kW)

Use the following Rated Power values:

  • DSAS: 1,300 kW
  • RRC: 660 kW
  • DFIG: 1,500 kW

Calculation example:

If the DFIG wind turbine generates 750 kW at a certain wind speed:

Power (p.u.) = 750 kW / 1,500 kW = 0.5 p.u.

Creating the comparative chart

📊 Chart generation

  1. Convert all data to p.u. values using the formulae above
  2. Create a chart with:
    • X axis: Wind Speed (m/s)
    • Y axis: Normalised power (p.u.)
  3. Plot the three curves on the same chart using different colours or line styles
  4. Add a legend identifying each curve (DSAS, RRC, DFIG)

Results analysis

🔍 Analysis questions

Once the curves are obtained, analyse the following aspects:

1. Comparison in Zone II (below rated speed):

  • Which wind turbine has the steepest curve? What does this mean?
  • Which one reaches 50% of its rated power first?
  • Which technology makes best use of low winds?

2. Rated wind speed:

  • At what speed does each wind turbine reach its rated power?
  • Why might this speed differ between technologies?
  • What advantages does reaching rated power at lower winds bring?

3. Shape of the curves:

  • Which curve is smoothest? What are the implications?
  • Do you observe differences in the transition between zones?
  • How does the control system affect the shape of the curve?

4. Conversion efficiency:

  • Which technology has the highest efficiency in the low wind zone?
  • Which technology is most efficient in the medium wind zone?
  • How would you explain these differences from a technical point of view?

💡 Physical interpretation

The differences observed in the curves are fundamentally due to:

  • Control system: Variable pitch vs fixed pitch vs variable speed control
  • Generator type: Squirrel cage, wound rotor, or doubly fed
  • Speed range: Fixed speed vs variable speed
  • Regulation strategy: Aerodynamic stall vs active control

Each technology represents a different trade-off between complexity, cost and performance.

Practical application

🌍 Relevance in real projects

Knowledge of these curves is essential for:

  • Wind turbine selection: Choosing the most appropriate technology according to the site's wind profile
  • Production estimation: Calculating the estimated annual energy by combining the power curve with the statistical wind distribution
  • Economic analysis: Evaluating profitability by considering investment vs expected production
  • Farm optimisation: Deciding the optimal mix of technologies in a wind farm

Conclusions and reflections

📝 Summary of key concepts

  • Power curves characterise the performance of each wind turbine
  • Normalisation (p.u. values) allows comparison of wind turbines of different sizes
  • The zone below rated speed is critical for annual energy production
  • Each technology has advantages at different wind speed ranges
  • The optimal selection depends on the specific characteristics of the site
