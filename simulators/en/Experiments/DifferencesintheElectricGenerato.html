Differences in the electric generators used in wind turbines

🎯 Learning objectives

Upon completing this topic you will be able to:

  • Identify the main types of electric generators used in wind turbines
  • Understand the fundamental differences between squirrel cage, wound rotor and doubly fed generators
  • Relate the generator type to the operational capabilities of the wind turbine
  • Evaluate the advantages and limitations of each generator technology

Introduction

The electric generator is the component that transforms the rotational mechanical energy of the rotor into electrical energy. The choice of generator type has a fundamental impact on:

  • The operating speed range of the wind turbine
  • The power control capability
  • The quality of the electricity generated
  • The complexity and cost of the system
  • Maintenance requirements

📖 Fundamental concept: Induction machines

All generators used in the wind turbine technologies we have studied are induction machines, also known as asynchronous machines.

These machines are characterised by:

  • Not requiring direct electrical contact between rotor and stator (in the case of squirrel cage)
  • Operating with a certain slip with respect to synchronous speed
  • Being robust, reliable and relatively inexpensive
  • Being able to operate both as a motor and as a generator

Historical evolution and main types

Throughout the evolution of wind technology, three main configurations of induction machines have been used:

⚙️ Types of induction generators in wind turbines

  1. Squirrel Cage Induction Generator (SCIG)
    • Direct grid connection
    • Practically fixed speed
    • Simplest and most economical technology
    • Used in first- and second-generation wind turbines
  2. Wound Rotor Generator with Rotor Resistance Control
    • Allows limited variable speed
    • Uses variable resistances in the rotor circuit
    • Intermediate technology in complexity and cost
    • Used in RRC wind turbines
  3. Doubly Fed Induction Generator (DFIG)
    • Allows wide variable speed range
    • Uses power electronic converters (back-to-back)
    • Most complex and sophisticated technology
    • Used in modern high-efficiency wind turbines

Relationship with wind turbine performance

🔗 Generator - overall performance connection

The type of generator used directly determines:

Wind turbine characteristic Generator dependency
Operating speed range SCIG: very limited (~1%)
Wound rotor: limited (~10%)
DFIG: wide (~30-40%)
Efficiency at partial loads Variable speed (DFIG) allows optimisation of tip speed ratio for each wind speed
Response to gusts Variable speed allows rotor inertia to be used as energy storage
Power quality DFIG offers decoupled control of active and reactive power
System complexity SCIG: minimum
Wound rotor: medium
DFIG: high
Cost SCIG: low
Wound rotor: medium
DFIG: high (but offset by higher production)

Current trends

🔮 Technology evolution

Today, most new wind turbines use DFIG technology or full converter systems, which offer:

  • Maximum operational flexibility
  • Optimal efficiency over a wide wind range
  • Excellent power quality
  • Grid support capability

However, squirrel cage and wound rotor generators remain relevant in:

  • Small and medium wind turbines
  • Applications where simplicity and low cost are priorities
  • Existing wind farms that continue to operate

Conclusion

📌 Key points to remember

  • The electric generator is a fundamental component that determines many operational characteristics of the wind turbine
  • There are three main types of induction machines used in wind turbines: squirrel cage, wound rotor and doubly fed
  • Technological evolution has progressed from fixed speed towards variable speed to improve efficiency and power quality
  • Each technology represents a trade-off between complexity, cost and performance
  • Understanding generators is essential to understanding the overall operation of the wind turbine
