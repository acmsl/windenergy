Why do the blades rotate?

Physical principles ››

The basic principles governing the forces acting on wind turbine blades and causing the hub to which they are connected to rotate are the same as those that enable aircraft to ascend. The rotation is due to the fact that the hub has only one degree of freedom: to rotate on its axis.

What are these forces?

Let's imagine we have an airplane wing in a wind tunnel, as shown in Fig. 1. In a wind tunnel, the air flow is uniform, so there is no turbulence (i.e., "laminar flow"). And we can vary its intensity at will.

The wing (represented by its section, delimited by the red line) is placed inside this wind tunnel, and we secure it firmly to the outside of the wind tunnel so that it cannot move. This is done with an internal bar that crosses the wing along its longest axis (the circle is shown in the center), allowing us to rotate it. In this way, we can change the angle of the wing's chord to the wind.

Fig 1.

The effect of the wind, coming from the left, on the wing is a compression of the flow on the underside of the blade and a depression on its upper side. The result is a set of forces applied along the surface of the blade: this set of forces is indicated by the small dotted arrows. 

Since the blade is a rigid body, the total effect of these forces can be assimilated to a single force whose value is the vector sum of all of them, and which is applied at the point of attachment, the bar represented by the circle: this force is indicated by the red vector. The magnitude and direction of this force depends on:

•the shape of the blade, 

•the speed of the incoming wind,

•the angle between the chord of the blade (gray line connecting the two ends of the blade) and the direction of the wind (arrow to the left). 

We can break down this red vector at will: in this particular case, it is convenient to think of this red vector as the sum of two forces: one to go up, called Flifting, and another that goes to the right, called Fdragging.

These two forces explain why the plane stays in the air (due to Flifting), and why it must be pushed to the left (to overcome Fdragging) in order for the plane to remain suspended in the air. This force is what the plane's engines must overcome.

Now let's replace the airplane wing with the blade of a wind turbine and place it in relation to the turbine's axis. The situation is similar, but reversed. See the following figure, which shows the view that an observer standing at the tip of one of the blades and looking at the hub axis would have. The wind would be in front of them and the blades would rotate to their left in a plane perpendicular to the screen and passing through the black vector.

Fig. 2.

In this case, the wind hitting the edge of the blade is the "Total Wind" (red vector), a combination of the incident wind (blue vector) and the rotational wind (black vector), which is produced by the blade as it rotates because it is at a certain distance from the point of rotation (the hub axis). We are assuming that the rotation occurs "to the left" and that, due to this movement, the blunt edge of the blade "cuts perpendicularly" through the incident wind. The speed of this cut is not uniform along the edge: it is lower the closer the section of the blade we are looking at is to the center of the hub (see Blades). This air cutting speed is equivalent to a wind blowing in the opposite direction with the blade remaining static. 

In this brief explanation, we will assume that the speed of this wind that interests us is the "average" speed along the entire edge.

The representation on the left is a way of solving the problem of representing the rotating blade, making the change of proposing a "view from the blade itself," as if we were sitting at the end of the blade looking towards the axis of rotation at the hub. The black vector would correspond to the wind we would feel on our face due to that rotation.

The total force is represented by the green vector, and similarly to the case of the wind tunnel and the airplane wing, we break it down into two forces: one that pulls the blade along its only degree of freedom (the plane of rotation), and another (light brown vector) that goes in the direction of the hub axis and is counteracted by the tower; that is, this is the force that tries to bend the tower.

This set of forces depends on the relative angle of the blade with respect to its plane of rotation: that is, in the figure above, on the angle between the red dotted line and the horizontal axis.
