Induction electric generators (actually induction electric machines, since they can be motors or generators) are characterized by a curve that shows the relationship between the mechanical torque on their rotating shaft and the rotational speed of this shaft. See the following figure.

Fig. 1: "Mechanical torque vs. rotational speed curve" of an induction electric machine.

There is a characteristic point on this graph defined by the generator's characteristic rotational speed, the synchronous speed. This speed corresponds to the rotational speed of the magnetic flux created in its air gap by the stator winding. To understand the two modes of operation, motor or generator, we must remember that this device transforms energy from one type to another (electrical energy into mechanical energy, or mechanical energy into electrical energy). Therefore, it "connects" two worlds:

•the rotating shaft, where the rotor winding is located, which must be mechanically connected to a device that can rotate and behave as a mechanical load or a mechanical energy generator.

•the stator winding, which must be connected to a source of electrical energy or an electrical energy consumer. 

The above graph considers the speed of rotation of the shaft, but does not say anything about how that speed was reached.

When the shaft speed is lower than the synchronous speed, a mechanical torque appears on the shaft, generated from electrical energy entering through the stator winding, provided that it is connected to a power source. 

 If we have somehow managed to make the shaft speed higher than the synchronous speed, and the shaft is connected to a device that provides mechanical torque (for example, the blades of a wind turbine) and the stator is connected to an electrical network, and the shaft speed has been made to be above the synchronous speed, then the mechanical torque applied to the shaft comes out of the stator as electrical energy.

Each phase of the grid is connected to a stator coil (one per color). The magnet (i.e., the magnetic flux) that is present corresponds to the vector sum of the magnetic fluxes created by each of the coils, fed by each of the phases of the grid.

When there is no mechanical load on the shaft (i.e., when the shaft is allowed to rotate freely), the rotational speed corresponds to the rotational speed of this equivalent magnet.

Figure 1 (above) shows that when friction is introduced on the shaft, its rotational speed decreases and the machine creates a mechanical torque that counteracts the friction. (Point A.)

On the other hand, if the shaft is forced to run at a speed higher than the synchronous speed, it consumes the mechanical torque necessary for this forcing, which appears as electrical energy in the stator coils. (Point B.) 

In other words, depending on whether we place a mechanical load on the shaft or supply mechanical torque, this machine behaves like a motor or a generator. 

A very important point to bear in mind is that the behavior of this machine changes depending on the speed of rotation: the area that interests us is the one around its synchronous speed.

The synchronous speed depends on the number of 'magnetic pole pairs' (1 in the case of the figure above) and the frequency of the network. In the case of the figure, and assuming 50 cycles/second, the synchronous speed is (50 cycles/second)/1, or 50 revolutions/second, or (50 * 60) revolutions/minute = (3,000 rpm).

Taking the synchronism speed as a reference, the operating curve (Fig. 1) usually uses the relative value of the shaft revolutions with respect to the synchronism revolutions, instead of the absolute value of the revolutions. This value is called slip (usually represented by the letter 's').

s = (ActualSpeed - SynchronismSpeed) / SynchronismSpeed 

or

s = (ActualSpeed / SynchronismSpeed) - 1
