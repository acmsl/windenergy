Structure of a Horizontal Axis Wind Turbine

What is a Wind Turbine? ››

A horizontal axis wind turbine (known as a HAWT) is an electric power generation plant consisting of a set of subsystems located in: the blades and hub, the nacelle, the tower, and the foundation.

•The blades are attached to the hub. They may be able to rotate along their longitudinal axis (variable pitch blades).

•The hub acts as a mechanical connection between the blades and the rotor shaft. The blades are connected to it either by a fixed connection (fixed-pitch wind turbines) or by a platform that allows them to rotate on their longitudinal axis (variable-pitch wind turbines). 

The nacelle is located on top of the tower, on a platform that allows it to rotate horizontally. The following components are located on this platform:

•The rotor bearing, which supports the weight of the blades and the hub, and from which the rotor shaft emerges.

•The rotor shaft, which mechanically connects the hub to the gearbox.

•The gearbox: a fixed-pitch gearbox with two shafts:

o input or low speed, connected to the Rotor Shaft; 

o and another, the output or high-speed shaft, which is connected to the generator shaft via a flexible coupling.

The above elements are known as the "DriveTrain."

•The Disc Brake, usually located on the high-speed shaft of the gearbox (generator side), although there are also brakes on the low-speed shaft (above the DriveTrain). It is used to:

oKeep the DriveTrain in a fixed position (stationary) when the wind turbine is being repaired.

oExceptionally, as a brake mechanism for the DriveTrain. 

•The flexible joint that connects the high-speed shaft of the gearbox to the generator shaft. This element solves the problems associated with the misalignment of these two shafts and the different mechanical stresses (sudden changes in wind, sudden changes in electrical load, etc.) that may occur. It also acts as a limiter of the mechanical torque received by the generator.

•The electric generator. There are several types, and it may be associated with power electronic devices.

In addition, there are auxiliary elements, such as:

•The yaw mechanism, which is responsible for changing the orientation of the nacelle. 

•The orientation locking mechanism. This mechanism is a brake that prevents the nacelle from rotating horizontally, which occurs when it is disoriented with respect to the wind.

•The wind vane, used to determine the difference between the wind direction and the orientation of the nacelle.

•The anemometer, which measures wind speed.

•The hydraulic brake control system.

•The lubrication system for the gearbox, which usually also includes an oil filtering and purification mechanism.

•Generator fans and gearbox lubrication system fans.

•Various sensors:

oTemperature sensors: for the generator, gearbox oil, rotor bearing, etc.

oRevolution measurement on the two shafts of the gearbox.

oPressure sensors: in the hydraulic brake circuit.

The power cables, which connect the generator to the grid connection at the bottom of the tower, run freely for a certain distance before being attached to the tower wall.

Depending on the wind turbine model, the nacelle may also contain:

•A capacitor bank, used to control the power factor delivered.

•The step-up transformer (from 690 V to medium voltage, typically 15 KV). This option has the advantage of reducing losses in the power cables by decreasing the current flowing through them.

From the point of view of the functional structure of a wind turbine, we can distinguish the following aspects:

•All mechanical, hydraulic, and electrical subsystems located in the nacelle or tower.

•The electrical power system, including the generator and electronic power converters, with their connection to the grid.

•The control unit (or controller), which is an electronic subsystem that ensures that all the above subsystems work in coordination, in accordance with safety and efficiency criteria in energy conversion.
To do this, it receives information from a set of sensors associated with the aforementioned subsystems and emits signals that control certain components within those other subsystems.
The Controller is responsible for ensuring that the Wind Turbine operates as an autonomous production plant: this is a distinguishing feature compared to other electricity production plants, which are usually operated by a team of local specialists. Once the "go-ahead" order has been received, the wind turbine itself checks whether the current wind conditions, power grid, temperatures, subsystem levels, vibrations, etc., are adequate, and if they are, it initiates the necessary operating sequences to connect to the power grid and deliver the energy produced in accordance with the instructions received from the customer. All this is done without requiring any information from any external device.
Similarly, when it receives the "cutout" order, it triggers a sequence that ends up disconnecting the wind turbine from the grid in an orderly manner.
The controller can receive "orders" through two channels: 

oThrough a communications system, usually connected to a SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) system, located either at the wind farm itself or at a remote location.

oA local connection, through which what is known as a "Control Terminal" is connected, a portable device used by Operations and Maintenance personnel to check the status of the wind turbine and to send control commands.
