Control of wind power collection

Physical principles ›› Concepts regarding extractable wind power ››

There are several methods for controlling the power collected from the wind: one method consists of controlling the angle of the blades (pitch), since varying this angle modifies the value of the aerodynamic coefficient (Cp). See Aerodynamic coefficient. This solution requires that the support for each blade on the hub must be able to rotate each blade on its longitudinal axis. This technique is known in the specialized literature as "Active Pitch." In English-language documentation, this method of pitch control is called "to the feathers," since to lower the Cp, the blade is made to "fold to the wind," that is, the angle of the blade is made to follow the wind, so that it offers the least surface area to it. 

There is another technique that also uses the pitch change mechanism but instead of "folding into the wind" it uses another effect (see Stalling). In the 3D Cp graphs, this area corresponds to the "hidden wall" which corresponds to negative pitch values. 

This control method is called "Active Stall" and is used in the wind turbine on which this simulator is based.

Implications for a wind turbine with a squirrel cage generator.

In the case of a squirrel cage induction machine used as a generator (as in this simulator), its operating speed is above, but very close to, its synchronous speed (see Introduction to induction machines). This synchronous speed, in most wind turbines, is typically between 1000 rpm and 3000 rpm, depending on the frequency of the grid and the number of poles of the generator.

In the particular case of the electric generator in this Simulator, there are two connections for the stator winding: one that "creates" 3 pairs of magnetic poles in the air gap, and another that creates 2 pairs of poles. The synchronous speeds when connected to a 50 Hz grid are therefore: 1000 rpm ( = 50 Hz * 60 / 3) in one case and 1500 rpm ( = 50 Hz * 60 / 2) in the other. 

The speed range at which this type of generator produces its maximum power is typically below 2% of the synchronous speed (see Characteristics of the generator emulated in this simulator), which is why wind turbines that use this type of generator are known as "fixed speed" turbines. Taking into account the first conclusion in the previous section, this implies that this wind turbine is not the best solution for harnessing winds that are below the nominal wind speed.

To mitigate this inadequacy, fixed-pitch wind turbines use the idea of having two generators that can operate at two different speeds, or a single generator that can operate at two speeds. This makes it possible to use one of them for winds close to or above the nominal wind speed and another that optimizes the TSR for winds well below the nominal wind speed.

In the case of this simulator, where the nominal wind speed is around 11 m/s, and the blade rotation speed for that power is 19.4 rpm, using the stator connection for synchronization at 1500 rpm (2 poles). Therefore, when the connection is changed to 3 poles, the wind for which the optimal TSR is reached is around 7.3 m/s (= 11 m/s * (1000 rpm / 1500 rpm)).

This technique has proven to be very efficient in practice, so this type of wind turbine was widely used before variable speed wind turbines.
