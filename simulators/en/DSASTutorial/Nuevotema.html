Description of a Thyristor

A thyristor is a solid-state semiconductor device that functions as a diode, i.e., current flows in one direction, from the anode to the cathode, provided that the voltage between the anode and cathode is positive, but only if the gate-cathode voltage has exceeded a certain value.

Typical circuit

The figure above shows an electrical circuit that can be used to control the light emitted by a bulb using a thyristor.

When switch S1 is closed, the circuit formed by resistor Rt and capacitor C reproduces the VAC signal at terminals C, but delayed in time. When this voltage at C exceeds the conduction value of diode D1 (approx. 0.6 V) plus the voltage required to excite the gate of the SCR thyristor (a value lower than "Forward break over"), the thyristor then starts to conduct. This is shown in the graph above on the right ("Conduction").

The longer the delay in exciting the thyristor gate, the shorter the time it will be conducting, and therefore the average current flowing through it in one cycle will be lower. The light emitted by the "Lamp" bulb is greater the higher this average current is. This delay is proportional to the values of Rt and C, i.e., the higher Rt is, the more the moment of entry into conduction will be delayed, and therefore the conduction time will be shorter. In other words, higher Rt ⇒ less light.

This is the same principle used in the Soft starter, except that in each phase of the Electrical Network two thyristors are placed in "anti-parallel," that is, connecting the cathode of one to the anode of the other, so that the circuit operates in both half-cycles.
