🎯 Analysis of generator changes

Objective: To understand why the system changes mode/generator and its effect on power and stability.

What to observe: Modes/states, power setpoint, rotor speed, and change criteria.

What to note: When the change occurs, what threshold triggers it, and how it stabilizes afterward.

Detailed analysis of the speed change mechanism (Small to Large Generator change)

Practical classes ››

This practical class aims to study the concepts surrounding the change in generator connection type (between 4 and 6 magnetic poles).

The basic reason is to make better use of the wind. As shown in Fig. 1 of Active Pitch (to-the-feathers), for a given blade, there is an optimal value between wind speed and blade rotation speed (TSRoptimo). 

Note that the ratio between the rotation speed of the generator shaft and that of the blades is fixed (the gear ratio). Furthermore, when in production, the generator remains very close to the synchronous speed. Check that with high wind (17 m/s) the power is nominal (1,300 kW) and the generator speed is 1,509.7 rpm: that is, the slip for maximum power is only 0.65% (= (1509.7 / 1500) -1). The result of these two observations is that the blades are forced to operate at a constant speed.

In order to make better use of the wind, the blades of this type of wind turbine are specially designed so that the area corresponding to the optimal TSR is not a single point but a wide range. In any case, this range does not cover a ratio between the nominal wind speed (around 14 m/s in the actual wind turbine) and a 'low' wind speed, for example 6 m/s, which are common.

The solution adopted in this wind turbine, common in several models from various manufacturers, consists of a 'gearbox' mechanism. In some models (not the one used for the simulator), there are two electric generators, connected to the blade shaft by means of a multiplier with two outputs. In the case of the wind turbine being simulated, the gearbox mechanism is based on having a generator with a 'chopped' stator winding, so that by changing the connection of these 'chunks' it is possible to have 2 pairs of poles (large generator) or 3 pairs of poles (small generator). These two cases correspond to synchronous speeds of 1500 rpm for 2 pairs, or 1000 rpm for 3 pairs. 

SynchronismSpeed = 50 Hz * 60 seconds / PairsOfPoles

There is another 'electrical' reason, which has to do with the 'power factor' characteristics of induction generators. In the characteristics tables, you can see that for the same active power, the power factor is better the closer the active power is to the rated power. In the case of the 2-pole connection, the rated power is 1300 kW, while with 3 poles, it drops to 350 kW. If we look at the power factor of the connection to 3 pole pairs for an active power of 250 kW, we see that it is 0.867, while generating these 250 kW with the connection to 2 pole pairs implies a power factor of 0.614.
